# Scope notice

Version 0.5, updated 30 September 2026. This public-data-only draft integrates the validated resting, visual, auditory, trial-level behavioral, developmental, and personalized-theta analyses. The ds006222 dataset is retained as a data-availability result because its public release supports a signal gate but not reconstruction of the prespecified behavioral or occlusion analyses. The separately frozen Dryad raw-EEG confirmation is complete in all 35 technical-pass participants and is reported alongside, but not conflated with, the 44-participant released-table reanalysis.

# Results

We first report cohort flow and dataset-specific findings, followed by tests of four predefined transitions from an observed result to a stronger mechanistic, cognitive, or predictive claim. Amplitudes and participants are not pooled across modalities or cohorts.

## Public-data cohort flow and signal availability

All 88 resting EEG recordings in ds004504 passed the structural checks and were matched without ambiguity to the corresponding visual-stimulation participant identifiers in ds006036. In the resting data, the median proportion of 4-s windows retained after the amplitude-based quality screen was 99.47%. The visual dataset contained 351 blocks at 5, 10, 15, or 20 Hz with at least 2.5 s of reconstructed eyes-open exposure, yielding 311 participant-by-frequency observations (Supplementary Figure S1). The numbers of participants with usable eyes-open responses were 80, 76, 78, and 77 at 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz, respectively; 68 participants contributed all four frequencies. The more restrictive network analysis retained 85 participants with at least one eligible window. Its median analysis duration was 2.862 s per block, corresponding to a median of six frequency-domain observations.

All 35 auditory recordings in ds005048 passed the file, channel, sampling-rate, duration, event, and finite-value checks. Eight participants had six stimulation blocks and 27 had ten blocks; the first six blocks were available for every participant and defined the common analysis interval. MMSE was available for 33 participants. The advanced auditory analysis therefore included 210 40-s stimulation blocks, with a median of 38 s and 57 frequency-domain observations retained per block.

Figure 1 maps each cohort to the four claim-transition tests and summarizes the evidence used to judge each proposed upgrade. Matrix cells denote a direct test, contextual evidence or a data boundary, or no direct role; they do not encode effect direction or evidence strength. Outcome-specific effective sample sizes and the reasons why technical and behavioral samples differed are summarized separately in Figure 2.

## Reference phenotypes, target engagement, and population sensitivity

### Resting EEG captured a cognitive-impairment-related spectral axis

The public resting dataset showed the expected relationship between spectral slowing and cognitive status (Figure 4A). Posterior individual alpha peak frequency correlated positively with MMSE (Spearman ρ=0.401, p=1.08×10⁻⁴), whereas the spectral slowing ratio correlated negatively with MMSE (ρ=−0.515, p=2.76×10⁻⁷). The spectral exponent (ρ=−0.452, p=9.62×10⁻⁶), relative delta power (ρ=−0.384, p=2.20×10⁻⁴), and relative theta power (ρ=−0.511, p=3.55×10⁻⁷) were also negatively related to MMSE, while relative alpha power was positively related (ρ=0.431, p=2.75×10⁻⁵). These estimates establish that the resting feature pipeline recovered a disease-related spectral pattern in the public cohort. Because MMSE was fixed at 30 in the control group, the correlations were not interpreted as an unqualified linear disease continuum.

### Eyes-open visual frequency-following responses were detectable but did not track MMSE

Eyes-open O1/O2 responses were positive in most participants at each stimulation frequency (Figure 3A). Median local signal-to-noise ratios were 4.659 dB at 5 Hz (64/80 participants above zero), 9.068 dB at 10 Hz (72/76), 8.777 dB at 15 Hz (76/78), and 8.206 dB at 20 Hz (70/77). The interquartile ranges were 1.211–7.060, 5.632–12.716, 5.882–11.474, and 4.559–11.648 dB, respectively. O1 and O2 had the largest or joint-largest median response at all four frequencies, consistent with an occipital-dominant scalp distribution (Supplementary Figure S2A). Applying a peak-to-peak threshold of 200 µV retained 334/351 blocks (95.2%) and left the frequency-specific medians materially unchanged.

The local response did not show a consistent monotonic relationship with MMSE. The unadjusted Spearman correlations were 0.087 at 5 Hz (p=0.441), −0.060 at 10 Hz (p=0.607), 0.043 at 15 Hz (p=0.707), and 0.081 at 20 Hz (p=0.482). Resting features also failed to predict the defined eyes-open local response after covariate adjustment and multiple-comparison control. Across seven resting predictors and four stimulation frequencies, the smallest false-discovery-rate-adjusted q value was 0.641. In particular, neither the distance between the individual alpha peak and 10 Hz (β=0.466 dB per SD, p=0.680) nor the resting slowing ratio (β=−0.024, p=0.971) predicted the 10 Hz local response.

### Closed-eye 10 Hz selectivity differed by diagnosis but was sensitive to response scale

The closed-eye driving-index pipeline reproduced the features released with the directly competing analysis. Across 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz, the Pearson correlation between locally generated and released features was at least 0.999978. This agreement establishes computational reproducibility of the feature extraction, not replication in an independent cohort.

At 10 Hz, the raw driving index was available for 80 participants. The means were 13.243 in cognitively normal controls (n=27), 6.493 in AD (n=32), and 6.618 in FTD (n=21); the corresponding medians were 9.520, 3.833, and 4.119. The large control-group standard deviation (16.053) relative to AD (6.337) and FTD (7.629) indicated marked right skew and sensitivity to high responders. In heteroskedasticity-consistent models, the adjusted raw-scale differences were −5.175 for AD versus controls (95% CI −10.730 to 0.381; p=0.0679) and −5.699 for FTD versus controls (95% CI −12.371 to 0.973; p=0.0941). After log1p transformation, the corresponding differences were −0.492 (95% CI −0.929 to −0.054; p=0.0276) and −0.595 (95% CI −1.136 to −0.053; p=0.0313). The raw and transformed estimates therefore agreed in direction but differed in inferential precision (Supplementary Figure S3A).

The participant-level frequency-selectivity contrast gave a more specific result (Figure 4B). Relative to the mean driving index at 5, 15, and 20 Hz, 10 Hz selectivity was lower in AD than in controls by 6.610 units (95% CI −11.876 to −1.345; p=0.0139) and lower in FTD by 7.174 units (95% CI −13.715 to −0.632; p=0.0316). A four-frequency random-intercept model produced similar 10 Hz contrasts, but the random-effect covariance was singular. The selectivity model therefore carries the primary group-level interpretation, with the repeated-frequency model treated as a sensitivity analysis.

All visual direct, transformed, selectivity, mixed, within-group severity, advanced, and reference-sensitivity results are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

## State robustness, within-recording reliability, and individual clinical validity

### The visual group effect did not establish individual clinical validity

Within the patient groups, the closed-eye 10 Hz driving index did not show a stable monotonic relationship with MMSE (Figure 4C). Spearman correlations were 0.125 in AD (95% bootstrap CI −0.233 to 0.442; n=32; p=0.497) and −0.237 in FTD (95% CI −0.611 to 0.221; n=21; p=0.302). Both intervals remained compatible with non-trivial associations. The directly competing analysis reported a split-half ICC of 0.06 and no improvement in diagnostic classification after the photic-driving features were added to resting EEG. Thus, the same response definition that produced a frequency-selective diagnostic-group contrast did not satisfy the available reliability, within-group severity, or incremental-value criteria for an individual biomarker.

The eyes-open and closed-eye analyses did not support a single state-invariant visual response effect. Eyes-open O1/O2 decibel signal-to-noise ratio was broadly detectable but showed no stable diagnostic or severity gradient, whereas the closed-eye posterior driving index showed a selective 10 Hz group difference. Eye state, analysis window, region of interest, and response denominator changed together, so their contributions could not be separated in this dataset.

### Auditory 40 Hz target engagement was stable within recordings but unrelated to MMSE

The auditory analysis showed a positive stimulation-minus-resting response in 32/35 participants (Figure 3A). The median narrowband 40 Hz signal-to-noise ratio increase was 1.345 dB (range −0.707 to 6.179 dB), with a broadly distributed sensor-level response (Supplementary Figure S2B). Participant rankings were stable across the first and second halves of the six common stimulation blocks (Spearman ρ=0.838 for the foundation-analysis response; Figure 3B). In the advanced analysis, the frontocentral response retained a split-half correlation of 0.752. These coefficients describe within-recording stability; they do not estimate between-day test–retest reliability.

Among the 33 participants with MMSE, the correlation between the narrowband 40 Hz response and MMSE was ρ=0.085 (95% bootstrap CI −0.283 to 0.417; p=0.637; Figure 4D). This interval was too wide to support equivalence to zero. The response also failed to show an ordinal diagnostic-stage pattern: the descriptive median was 1.829 dB in the normal group, 2.492 dB in MCI, and 0.871 dB in mild AD. Given the small and unbalanced groups, the diagnostic-label/MMSE discordance, and a single participant with moderate AD, these summaries were not used to infer a stage trajectory.

All auditory adjusted, reference-sensitivity, amplitude-threshold, leave-one-subject-out, and within-recording split-half results are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

### Network and temporal measures did not add clinical information

Visual network and temporal candidates were constrained by the short usable windows. Participant rankings for posterior–frontal imaginary coherence were weakly consistent across reference schemes (Supplementary Figure S3B), and none of the prespecified patient-severity models survived false-discovery-rate correction (minimum q=0.870). The primary diagnostic models had a minimum q of 0.800. Thirty-two exploratory models relating resting features to advanced visual measures also failed to survive correction (minimum q=0.110; Supplementary Figure S4). The overlap between nominal early and late 2-s windows had a median of 1.138 s, precluding a strong interpretation of within-block adaptation.

The 28 basic resting-to-visual models and 32 advanced models are reported as separate false-discovery-rate families in Supplementary Table S5.

The longer auditory blocks supported more stable estimation of some advanced measures. Split-half correlations were 0.591 for the central-minus-frontal signal-to-noise gradient and 0.529 for current-source-density-transformed central–frontal absolute imaginary coherence. After adjustment for age, sex, and the local frontocentral response, neither measure was associated with MMSE: the coefficients per 1-SD higher MMSE were −0.059 for the spatial gradient (p=0.810, q=0.811) and −0.00063 for imaginary coherence (p=0.811, q=0.811). The split-half correlations were −0.191 for the fourth-minus-first-quarter response and −0.204 for the within-block slope. Reference and leave-one-subject-out checks are shown in Supplementary Figure S3C–D. No exploratory auditory connectivity or adaptation measure passed false-discovery-rate correction; the minimum q was 0.883 across the full exploratory family and 0.995 in the subset testing incremental value beyond the local response (Supplementary Figure S4A).

## Matched behavioral validity and held-out incremental prediction

### Aggregate reliability did not guarantee matched concurrent validity

The ds007648 release contained 22 participants and 10,258 behavioral trials. Two recordings were excluded from neural analyses because the released signal files were truncated or frame-misaligned; the remaining 20 participants contributed 8,579 trials to the primary accuracy analysis and 7,456 correct trials to the response-time analysis. These exclusions were based on file integrity and signal availability rather than behavioral performance or response magnitude.

Participant rankings were stable for both aggregate frequency-tagged responses. Odd–even trial correlations, corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula, were 0.870 for 36 Hz occipital inter-trial phase coherence and 0.985 for 40 Hz central inter-trial phase coherence (Figure 3B). In matched participant-level analyses, 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence was not clearly associated with aggregate accuracy (Spearman ρ=−0.056, 95% participant-bootstrap CI −0.511 to 0.435; p=0.816) or median correct-trial response time (ρ=0.113, 95% CI −0.350 to 0.515; p=0.636). The intervals were wide because only 20 participants contributed neural data.

Aggregate behavioral rankings were also stable within the recording. Spearman–Brown-corrected reliability was 0.912 for participant-level accuracy (95% participant-bootstrap CI 0.730 to 0.971) and 0.991 for median correct-trial response time (95% CI 0.950 to 0.999). Thus, the aggregate neural and behavioral reliability coefficients and the aggregate concurrent-validity estimates refer to the same between-participant level.

The trial-level analysis addressed a different estimand. The leave-one-trial-out 40 Hz phase-projected score was not associated with accuracy (β=−0.0437 log-odds per within-participant SD, 95% CI −0.1071 to 0.0197; p=0.177, q=0.353) or correct-trial log response time (β=−0.00179, 95% CI −0.01497 to 0.01139; p=0.790, q=0.790). Aggregate inter-trial phase-coherence reliability cannot be assigned to this trial-state predictor. Target-modality interactions and amplitude-based alternatives did not alter the trial-level result.

In contrast, greater trial-level alignment with the participant-specific 36 Hz phase was associated with higher subsequent accuracy (β=0.1148 log-odds per within-participant SD, 95% CI 0.0466 to 0.1830; p=0.00097, q=0.00386), corresponding to an odds ratio of approximately 1.12 per SD (Figure 5A–C). The estimate was similar under a stricter ocular-artifact rule and after exclusion of the participant with late-recording noise. The 36 Hz association was not clearly specific to visual targets, was not reproduced by the amplitude measure, and did not meet the corrected threshold for response time. It therefore identifies a concurrent phase-state association rather than an effect of experimentally increasing 36 Hz response strength.

Leave-one-participant-out prediction did not convert either frequency-tagged signal into useful incremental prediction (Figure 6C). Adding the neural measures changed accuracy log loss by −0.000238 (95% bootstrap CI −0.000711 to 0.000309) and log-response-time mean absolute error by 0.000052 (95% CI −0.000235 to 0.000327). Relative to the corresponding baseline errors, these differences were −0.067% (95% CI −0.201% to 0.088%) and 0.012% (95% CI −0.052% to 0.073%), respectively. Thus, the reliable 40 Hz response did not show detectable concurrent behavioral validity in this task, while the 36 Hz concurrent association did not produce detectable out-of-participant predictive gain.

### Developmental sensitivity was separable from matched cognitive validity

Of 123 participants with auditory steady-state recordings in ds006780, 111 met the signal-based criterion of at least 100 valid standard trials at both 27 and 40 Hz; 109 also had full-scale IQ data. The technical sample comprised 54 autistic participants, 33 typically developing participants, and 24 unaffected siblings. Odd–even trial correlations, corrected to full length, were 0.951 at 27 Hz and 0.972 at 40 Hz.

Reconstructed d-prime was likewise reliable within the session. Spearman–Brown-corrected coefficients were 0.916 for the 27 Hz standard condition (95% participant-bootstrap CI 0.863 to 0.949) and 0.931 for the 40 Hz standard condition (95% CI 0.884 to 0.959). The split preserved the exact full-task hit, miss, false-alarm, and correct-rejection totals used in the primary analysis.

The metric-matched validity analysis used 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence, which had a full-length reliability of 0.972, and the reliable 40 Hz d-prime outcome. After adjustment for 27 Hz inter-trial phase coherence, age, sex, and diagnostic group, the coefficient was 0.058 d-prime units per 1-SD higher 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence (95% CI −0.255 to 0.370; p=0.717). On the standardized outcome scale, the estimate was 0.047 SD per neural-predictor SD (90% CI −0.167 to 0.261). The interval excluded effects of 0.30 outcome SD or larger under the non-clinical sensitivity grid (TOST p=0.026), but did not exclude 0.25-SD effects (p=0.059). This directly aligned analysis did not establish concurrent validity despite high within-recording reliability of both variables, but its precision did not support a strict null interpretation.

The analysis recovered the expected developmental effect (Figure 3D). Per 1-SD increase in age, 40 Hz local log signal-to-noise ratio increased by 2.258 dB (95% CI 1.104 to 3.413; p=0.000126, q=0.00114), and 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence increased by 0.0753 (95% CI 0.0421 to 0.1085; p=8.80×10⁻⁶, q=0.000158). Adjusted diagnostic-group differences did not survive multiplicity correction.

Developmental sensitivity did not imply concurrent task validity (Figure 5D). In the prespecified model controlling for 27 Hz response, age, sex, and diagnostic group, the standardized 40 Hz local log signal-to-noise ratio was unrelated to 40 Hz d-prime (β=−0.004, 95% CI −0.313 to 0.305; p=0.979, q=0.979). Its standardized 90% interval was −0.215 to 0.208 outcome SD, excluding effects of 0.25 SD or larger under the sensitivity grid (TOST p=0.027). Morlet power and inter-trial phase coherence gave similarly imprecise estimates. The inter-trial phase-coherence result is the metric-matched reliability–validity test; the local signal-to-noise ratio result does not inherit inter-trial phase-coherence reliability or its sensitivity bound. Repeated cross-validation showed higher prediction error after neural features were added: mean absolute error increased by 0.0146 (95% bootstrap CI 0.0060 to 0.0232), and root mean squared error increased by 0.0200 (95% CI 0.0116 to 0.0286). These changes represented relative deteriorations of 1.48% (95% CI 0.61% to 2.36%) and 1.70% (95% CI 0.99% to 2.45%), respectively.

An exploratory conditional association with full-scale IQ required a separate interpretation (Figure 6A–B). In the full technically usable release, a 1-SD higher 40 Hz local log signal-to-noise ratio was associated with a 5.13-point lower full-scale IQ after the same adjustment set (95% CI −9.49 to −0.78; p=0.0209, q=0.0418). The direction was consistent for Morlet power and inter-trial phase coherence. However, the local signal-to-noise estimate weakened to −2.98 points (95% CI −7.12 to 1.17; p=0.159) when the analysis was restricted to participants with full-scale IQ above 80, and to −2.49 points (95% CI −6.64 to 1.66; p=0.239) under the combined age and IQ eligibility range. Adding the neural features did not clearly improve out-of-sample full-scale IQ prediction. The association therefore depended on the released sample's ability range and did not qualify as a transportable cognitive marker.

## Mean active-control behavioral effect, within-participant coupling, and individual selection

### Personalized theta stimulation produced reliable target engagement and an acute performance advantage without condition-specific neural–behavioral coupling

The Dryad personalized-theta cohort included 44 adults aged 60–75 years who completed individualized theta-frequency stimulation, stimulation at 1.33 times the individualized frequency, fixed 2 Hz stimulation, and a non-rhythmic condition. Analyses of the released participant-level table reproduced an active-control advantage: the mean of the two personalized conditions was 10.56 ms faster than 2 Hz stimulation (95% CI −16.25 to −4.86; p=0.000544; paired d<sub>z</sub>=−0.563). The individualized and 1.33-times-individualized conditions differed by −1.28 ms (95% CI −5.20 to 2.64; p=0.515). Accuracy was 0.43 percentage points lower under the personalized conditions (95% CI −0.85 to −0.003 percentage points; p=0.0483), but the inverse-efficiency difference remained favorable (−8.44 ms, 95% CI −14.21 to −2.68; p=0.00510). The response-time advantage therefore persisted after an accuracy correction, although a small speed–accuracy contribution could not be excluded.

Decomposing the released entrainment measure separated between-participant and within-participant associations. After adjustment for condition, baseline response time, age, and sex, a 1-SD higher participant-level mean entrainment value was associated with a 12.25-ms faster post-stimulation response (95% CI −20.16 to −4.33; p=0.00242). The condition-specific deviation from each participant's mean was −0.90 ms per SD (95% CI −3.08 to 1.28; p=0.417), and the same within-participant estimate remained imprecise in a participant-fixed-effect model and a 20,000-permutation test (p=0.402). The released measure may therefore identify a stable between-participant response tendency, but it did not provide evidence that condition-to-condition increases mediated the behavioral advantage.

Claims about differential benefit also depended on the comparator. Baseline response time correlated with improvement relative to the participant's own baseline (r=0.349, 95% CI 0.058 to 0.585; p=0.020) and with advantage over the non-rhythmic condition (r=−0.475, 95% CI −0.676 to −0.207; p=0.00112), but not with the personalized advantage over active 2 Hz stimulation (r=−0.133, 95% CI −0.414 to 0.170; p=0.389). A nested leave-one-participant-out ridge model using pretreatment age, sex, baseline response time, and individualized theta frequency had a mean absolute error of 14.64 ms, compared with 14.56 ms for the training-set-mean benchmark (difference 0.08 ms, 95% bootstrap CI −0.32 to 0.49), a relative difference of 0.57% (95% CI −2.18% to 3.39%). These released-table analyses did not support a deployable individual-benefit rule and motivated the independently reconstructed raw-EEG tests below.

In the frozen 35-participant confirmatory cohort, frontocentral ITPC at the common analysis frequency was higher after theta-plus than after non-rhythmic stimulation (mean paired difference 0.088, 95% CI 0.051 to 0.124; paired d<sub>z</sub>=0.830; two-sided p=2.23×10<sup>−5</sup>; Figure 7A). The estimate was unchanged when at least four good frontocentral channels were required. Evoked local log SNR was also higher by 7.518 dB (95% CI 4.522 to 10.514; d<sub>z</sub>=0.862; Holm-adjusted p=3.83×10<sup>−5</sup>), whereas total-power (0.306 dB, 95% CI −0.097 to 0.709; Holm-adjusted p=0.264) and induced-power (−0.097 dB, 95% CI −0.393 to 0.200; Holm-adjusted p=0.512) contrasts crossed zero. This dissociation localized the regularity effect to phase-consistent activity under the tested decomposition; it does not distinguish endogenous entrainment from repeated or temporally predictable evoked responses.

ITPC showed high within-recording internal consistency in both theta-plus (Spearman–Brown coefficients 0.956 for odd–even and 0.961 for time-half splits) and non-rhythmic conditions (0.964 and 0.946, respectively). Evoked-SNR consistency was lower in theta-plus (0.855 and 0.717) and weak and imprecise in the non-rhythmic condition (0.135 and 0.236). These coefficients characterize within-session participant ordering and do not establish between-day stability.

Theta-plus stimulation reduced median correct response time by 27.727 ms relative to the non-rhythmic control (95% CI −36.171 to −19.284; d<sub>z</sub>=−1.128; Holm-adjusted p=3.49×10<sup>−7</sup>) and reduced inverse efficiency by 25.856 ms (95% CI −35.455 to −16.257; d<sub>z</sub>=−0.925; Holm-adjusted p=8.31×10<sup>−6</sup>; Figure 7B). The accuracy difference was −0.006 (95% CI −0.014 to 0.002; Holm-adjusted p=0.119), providing no detected accuracy decrement but not a formal non-inferiority result.

The stronger neural response did not establish condition-specific neural–behavioral coupling (Figure 7C). For leave-one-trial-out phase alignment centered within participant and condition, correctness slopes were −0.098 in non-rhythmic trials (95% CI −1.187 to 0.991) and −0.072 in theta-plus trials (95% CI −0.879 to 0.735); correct-log-response-time slopes were 0.006 (95% CI −0.029 to 0.042) and −0.004 (95% CI −0.026 to 0.019), respectively. All Holm-adjusted p values were 1.000. These estimates neither supported concurrent coupling nor tested mediation.

Adding the frozen neural features did not consistently improve nested leave-one-participant-out prediction of inverse-efficiency benefit (Figure 7D). Relative to the training-fold mean, the mean absolute-error improvement was 3.874 ms (95% bootstrap CI −0.431 to 8.032) and the root-mean-square-error improvement was 2.793 ms. Relative to the baseline-feature model, the corresponding improvements were −1.024 ms (95% CI −3.207 to 1.013) and 0.225 ms. Because improvement over both comparators was not established, the prespecified prediction gate did not pass. This is a sample-bounded failure of incremental prediction, not evidence that individual benefit is intrinsically unpredictable.

## Cross-cohort tests of four inferential transitions

The seven datasets provided four design-based tests of whether evidence at one level justified a stronger claim (Table 2). First, the visual data showed that a detectable or diagnostically sensitive frequency-following response was conditional on eye state, response scale, spatial summary, and denominator; the available evidence did not support a state-general endogenous-entrainment interpretation. Second, two independent analyses paired high within-recording neural reliability with weak or imprecise metric-matched cognitive validity: auditory 40 Hz signal-to-noise ratio versus MMSE in ds005048, and 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence versus 40 Hz d-prime in ds006780. Third, the personalized-theta cohort showed a mean advantage over active 2 Hz stimulation without corresponding evidence for condition-level neural–behavioral coupling. Fourth, in-sample associations in ds007648, ds006780, and the personalized-theta cohort did not consistently improve held-out prediction. These results constrain the four proposed upgrades without negating the positive evidence at their preceding levels.

# Discussion

Across the public datasets, rhythmic sensory responses were usually detectable and often stable within a recording, but each stronger interpretation required a separate test (Table 2). The analyses identified four recurrent breaks in the evidential chain: detectability or group sensitivity did not establish a state-general mechanism; high within-recording reliability did not establish matched cognitive validity; a mean active-control behavioral advantage did not establish within-participant neural coupling; and an in-sample association did not establish incremental prediction for new participants. These are bounded counterexamples to automatic claim upgrading, not evidence that rhythmic stimulation lacks physiological or behavioral effects. Closed-eye 10 Hz selectivity differed by diagnostic group, 40 Hz responses were highly reliable in three independent cohorts, a trial-level 36 Hz phase measure was associated with accuracy, and personalized theta conditions produced a mean response-time advantage over an active rhythmic control. The contribution of the cross-cohort analysis is to locate where those positive findings stopped supporting stronger biomarker, mechanistic, or treatment-selection claims.

The closed-eye 10 Hz result illustrates why a group contrast is insufficient for biomarker qualification. Its frequency-selective diagnostic difference was reproducible within the same data, but the raw-scale estimate was imprecise, the split-half ICC reported for the driving index was 0.06, and the within-AD and within-FTD correlations with MMSE were weak and directionally inconsistent. A diagnostic-group mean can differ when individual distributions overlap substantially or when within-person measurement error is large. Such an effect may remain useful for understanding a stimulus-response phenotype, but it does not support individual classification, staging, or treatment selection without additional reliability and external-validation evidence.

The visual results were also conditional on state and metric. The closed-eye posterior driving index and eyes-open O1/O2 decibel signal-to-noise ratio differed simultaneously in eye condition, analysis interval, spatial summary, and denominator. The observed contrast between their results cannot therefore be attributed to eye closure alone. It instead sets a broader reproducibility requirement: the state, frequency, reference, region of interest, and response definition must be fixed before a stimulation biomarker is evaluated. This requirement is consistent with evidence that individual gamma center frequencies in older adults may fall below 40 Hz [L031] and with methodological work showing that a stimulus-frequency peak need not indicate entrainment of an endogenous oscillator [L001, L007]. A visual temporal-prediction experiment provides a direct active-control example: delta phase concentration was comparable for a periodic stream and a less-periodic repeated-interval stream that was similarly predictive, despite a computational entrainment model predicting stronger phase concentration for the periodic condition [L041]. The young healthy sample and delta-band task do not establish the corresponding result in ageing, cognitive impairment, or gamma stimulation; the study instead shows why phase concentration alone is not a mechanism-specific endpoint.

The resting-to-stimulation analyses further separate disease-associated spontaneous activity from the measured sensory response. Resting alpha peak frequency, slowing, spectral exponent, and band power tracked MMSE in ds004504, yet these features did not predict the defined eyes-open local visual response after adjustment and multiple-comparison control. This result applies to the tested features and response definition; it does not establish biological independence between spontaneous and evoked activity. It does show that a disease-related resting feature cannot be assumed to predict a stimulation response without a direct, prespecified test in linked participants.

The auditory cohorts provide complementary boundaries rather than direct replications of the visual results. The older-adult 40 Hz cohort yielded a stable local response and estimable non-zero-lag measures, yet response magnitude, spatial gradient, imaginary coherence, and adaptation did not show incremental associations with MMSE. The healthy-young and developmental cohorts extended that boundary to behavior and out-of-sample prediction. In ds007648, highly reliable aggregate 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence had imprecise, near-zero associations with aggregate accuracy and response time; the separate trial-state analysis was interpreted from its own clustered interval and was not assigned aggregate reliability. In ds006780, both aggregate inter-trial phase coherence and d-prime were reliable, but their adjusted association was imprecise and crossed zero. The primary local signal-to-noise ratio analysis remained a separate validity result because corresponding reliability was not estimated. Absolute amplitudes cannot be compared across cohorts because participants, stimuli, systems, references, and response definitions differed. Their common evidence concerns the ordering of validation claims when metric and aggregation level are held constant: detectability and reliability did not guarantee concurrent or predictive validity.

The now-published ds006222 source study reported better vigilance accuracy and response time in the 40 Hz audiovisual group than in constant-light and random-flicker groups, together with exploratory cross-participant relations involving delta power and lower-alpha connectivity [L044]. Those results are relevant positive acute-behavior evidence, but the public package available for this analysis omitted the separate PsychoPy outcome files needed to reconstruct the behavioral exclusions and contrasts. We therefore neither treated the missing endpoints as a null result nor counted the article's associations as independently reproduced. This cohort supplies a distinct reproducibility boundary: a public EEG release can support target-engagement verification while remaining insufficient for independent qualification of the linked behavioral endpoint.

The personalized-theta analysis showed positive neural and behavioral effects within the same cohort. In the raw-EEG confirmation, temporal regularity produced a large phase-locking difference, a selectively evoked spectral response, and faster performance with favorable inverse efficiency. Yet trial-to-trial phase alignment did not track correctness or response time within either condition, and participant-level neural features did not reliably add held-out prediction. The experiment therefore provides a same-cohort demonstration that target engagement, acute benefit, concurrent coupling, and individual prediction are separate evidentiary transitions. The absence of supported coupling does not negate the neural or behavioral effects; it limits the claim that the measured phase response is their condition-specific behavioral mechanism.

This same-cohort separation provides an empirical complement to the recent distinction between physiological resonance and artificial superimposition [L043]. The selective evoked response observed here is compatible with phase-consistent stimulus following, but the present design cannot establish endogenous resonance, deep-brain engagement, or the biological mechanisms proposed for repeated 40 Hz treatment. More importantly for human translation, even robust target engagement did not make the measured phase response a validated proxy for the behavioral advantage. Mechanism specificity and cognitive-marker validity therefore require related but non-interchangeable tests.

Comparator choice changed the apparent heterogeneity of personalized benefit. Slower baseline performance was related to change from the participant's own baseline and to advantage over the non-rhythmic condition, but not to added benefit over fixed 2 Hz stimulation. Baseline-adjusted change scores and inactive comparators can therefore answer a different question from treatment selection between rhythmic alternatives. The failed nested prediction model imposes a further boundary: an average active-control effect does not show that pretreatment variables can select the better stimulation setting for a new participant.

The negative and unstable cognitive associations also require interval- and population-aware interpretation. The ds006780 full-release association with full-scale IQ was directionally consistent across three response measures, but it weakened under the ability range used in the source study and did not yield clear predictive improvement. This is not evidence that the full-sample estimate was spurious, nor that 40 Hz activity impairs cognition. It shows that eligibility range, comparator frequency, concurrent association, and prediction define different estimands. Reporting only the most favorable association would obscure that transportability problem. The same distinction applies to the recent open-label personalized-gamma pilot in early AD, in which gamma center-frequency change correlated with cognitive preservation and baseline EEG features differed between post hoc response groups [L040]. With 16 treated participants, no concurrent control, and no held-out responder validation, that study motivates prospective stratification but does not yet establish an individual treatment-selection marker.

This distinction also constrains therapeutic interpretation. In OVERTURE, EEG-verified target engagement occurred without separation on the principal cognitive outcomes, while a selected MRI subset produced secondary structural signals [L027, L035]. A subsequent systematic review and meta-analysis reported the same endpoint-level tension: structural outcomes showed a pooled signal, whereas cognition and activities of daily living did not show clear benefit [L013]. These findings are relevant but should not be collapsed into a single efficacy claim or used to qualify EEG synchronization as a surrogate. The larger HOPE pivotal trial had completed follow-up but had not posted results at the literature cutoff [L036]. A newly registered randomized double-blind trial now compares 40 Hz with non-40 Hz music in older adults with cognitive decline and designates EEG spectral, gamma-response, and connectivity measures as primary outcomes, with memory and global cognitive outcomes assessed longitudinally [L042]. Because that trial is recruiting and has not posted results, it provides no efficacy evidence; it does, however, make the validation status of its EEG endpoints a prospective clinical question rather than a purely methodological one. The present public datasets include acute behavior but no longitudinal treatment outcome and cannot determine whether repeated sensory stimulation alters cognition or disease progression. They instead identify requirements that should precede a surrogate-endpoint claim: state-robust target engagement, between-day reliability, a prespecified within-participant relationship with a clinically meaningful outcome, and out-of-sample evidence that the marker adds information beyond established predictors.

Event timing was reconstructed from hardware and eye-state markers rather than nominal zero-duration annotations. Technical exclusions were separated from behavioral outcomes, and public-data results include response-scale, reference, artifact, eligibility-range, comparator, complete-case, and behavioral-reliability analyses. Group effects, within-group severity, within-recording stability, concurrent association, the active-control behavioral effect, condition-level coupling, and out-of-sample prediction were treated as separate targets. The 10 Hz feature pipeline was reproduced to near-numerical identity with the released competing analysis, and the same-data status was retained explicitly.

Several limitations constrain these conclusions. The analyses are secondary and cannot be described as prospective confirmation, although the 35-participant Dryad analysis was frozen before confirmatory signal re-access. Most cohorts differed in age, diagnosis, task, modality, reference, and response definition, so cross-cohort consistency applies to validation logic rather than a pooled biological effect. MMSE is a coarse outcome with a ceiling in the visual control group and inconsistent diagnostic labels in the older-adult auditory cohort. The visual windows were sufficient for local frequency-following estimates but short for connectivity and temporal dynamics. Split-half coefficients for both neural and behavioral measures do not replace between-day test–retest reliability. Trial-level neural–behavioral associations remain vulnerable to time-varying common causes, and null intervals do not prove absence of all neural–behavioral relationships. The acute personalized-theta cohort comprised healthy older adults rather than patients with cognitive impairment. The available data therefore do not support anatomical source, directed propagation, endogenous-oscillator entrainment, chronic-treatment efficacy, disease modification, or patient treatment selection.

Human rhythmic-sensory EEG responses were measurable across populations, but their meaning depended on state, frequency, response definition, comparator, and the claim being tested. A diagnostic-group contrast could coexist with low individual reliability, a highly reliable response could lack behavioral validity, and an average active-control benefit could coexist with weak condition-level neural coupling and unsuccessful individual-benefit prediction. Sensory target engagement is therefore an initial observation, not evidence by itself of a cognitive biomarker, treatment-selection marker, or clinical surrogate.
