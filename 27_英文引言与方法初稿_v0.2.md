# Working title

From target engagement to cognitive inference: testing four inferential transitions across seven human rhythmic-sensory EEG cohorts

# Introduction

Rhythmic sensory stimulation can elicit large stimulus-locked electroencephalographic responses without invasive intervention. This property has motivated its use in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), particularly at 40 Hz, as both a physiological probe and a candidate therapeutic approach. Human studies have established that visual, auditory, and combined audiovisual stimulation can generate responses at the driving frequency in healthy adults and in patients with cognitive impairment [L003, L014, L020]. The interpretation of those responses remains less settled. A spectral peak at the stimulation frequency establishes that the recorded signal follows the stimulus, but it does not by itself show that an endogenous oscillator has been entrained. Source-resolved magnetoencephalography has shown that visually driven and endogenous gamma responses can arise from distinct, coexisting generators [L007], and methodological analyses have described several mechanisms by which repeated evoked responses produce apparent phase locking [L001]. In a direct temporal-prediction comparison, delta phase concentration was similar for periodic visual streams and less-periodic repeated-interval streams that provided comparable timing information, although an oscillatory entrainment model predicted stronger concentration for the periodic stream [L041]. We therefore use *frequency-following response* or *target engagement* for the observed EEG effect and reserve *entrainment* for designs that test stronger dynamical criteria.

The distinction is clinically consequential because stimulus-locked responses, diagnostic group differences, individual biomarkers, and surrogate endpoints require different evidence. A response can be detectable yet remain conditional on state or metric. High within-recording reliability may coexist with weak cognitive validity, and a mean behavioral effect may not be accompanied by within-participant neural coupling. An in-sample association may also fail to improve prediction in held-out participants. These inferential transitions are often compressed into a single claim about “gamma entrainment,” although each has a separate estimand and validation requirement.

A recent mechanistic synthesis identified the distinction between physiological resonance and artificial superimposition as a central unresolved determinant of deep-brain engagement and therapeutic outcome [L043]. That distinction is conceptually important but does not itself specify how a human EEG response should be qualified as a cognitive or predictive marker. The present study operationalizes the problem as a sequence of empirical claim transitions, asking at each step whether the available human data justify moving from a measured response to a stronger mechanistic, cognitive, or individualized interpretation.

The human literature already contains examples of these separations. Forty-hertz auditory steady-state responses have shown good long-term reliability in a small healthy sample recorded across two clinical EEG systems [L032]. In AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), however, an earlier case-control study reported higher 40 Hz auditory response power in AD than in MCI and controls, with a moderate association with ADAS-Cog [L016]. Visual studies have yielded a different pattern: stimulus-induced endogenous gamma was attenuated in small MCI and AD subgroups, whereas the steady-state visually evoked response was not [L017]. More recently, a secondary analysis of a public photostimulation cohort reported selective attenuation of 10 Hz photic driving in AD and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), but the same measure had near-null split-half reliability and did not improve classification beyond resting EEG [L024]. These findings are not necessarily contradictory. Auditory steady-state responses, visual frequency-following responses, and task-induced gamma differ in their generators, stimulation parameters, brain state, and measurement definitions.

The common use of a fixed 40 Hz stimulus adds another source of heterogeneity. In 32 cognitively normal older adults tested with visual stimulation from 32 to 40 Hz, individual center frequencies were most often 32 or 34 Hz, and connectivity measures were stronger or more stable at the individual center frequency than at the other tested frequencies [L031]. A subsequent open-label study in 16 amyloid-positive participants with early AD reported that change in gamma center frequency after 12 weeks of individually optimized 32–40 Hz stimulation correlated with cognitive preservation [L040]. That uncontrolled pilot is hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of treatment efficacy or a validated responder rule, but it makes the distinction between association, stratification, and out-of-sample prediction especially consequential. Eye state, waveform, recording reference, region of interest, stimulation duration, and the response denominator may further alter the observed effect. Claims about a general capacity for “gamma entrainment” are therefore difficult to evaluate unless the state and metric are specified.

Clinical trials reinforce the need to separate target engagement from benefit. Small feasibility and pilot studies have shown that daily 40 Hz audiovisual stimulation can be delivered to patients with mild AD and can produce an online EEG response; exploratory imaging, activity, and memory outcomes have also been reported [L003, L026]. In the six-month randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled OVERTURE study, active treatment produced EEG-verified gamma target engagement, but the groups did not separate on MADCOMS, CDR-SB, or ADAS-Cog14 [L027]. A selected MRI subset from the same trial showed signals in white-matter and myelin-related outcomes [L035]. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis likewise reported a pooled signal for structural outcomes but no clear improvement in cognition or activities of daily living [L013]. These findings address different endpoint levels and do not validate an intermediate physiological response as a surrogate. The 673-participant HOPE pivotal study completed follow-up in August 2026 after testing 12-month cognitive-functional efficacy, but the registry had not posted results as of 3 October 2026 [L036]. The clinical efficacy of gamma sensory stimulation therefore remains an empirical question; an online EEG response cannot substitute for the pending outcome evidence.

We examined this measurement chain across seven public human EEG datasets. Linked resting and visual photostimulation recordings provided disease-related resting features and within-participant resting-to-stimulation tests in AD, FTD, and normal cognition. Independent auditory and audiovisual datasets contributed 40 Hz detectability, within-recording stability, trial-level behavior, developmental sensitivity, general cognitive performance, and data-availability controls. A within-participant study in older adults added individualized theta-frequency stimulation, an active 2 Hz comparator, post-stimulation behavior, and an author-derived neural response measure.

The cross-cohort synthesis tested four inferential transitions. First, we asked whether a detectable or group-sensitive frequency-following response was sufficiently state- and metric-robust to support a general mechanistic interpretation. Second, we tested whether high within-recording reliability was accompanied by metric- and level-matched cognitive validity. Third, we separated a mean active-control behavioral effect from within-participant neural–behavioral coupling. Fourth, we evaluated whether in-sample neural associations added predictive information for held-out participants. Detectability, experimental or developmental sensitivity, concurrent validity, a mean active-control behavioral effect, within-participant coupling, and individual prediction remained distinct estimands. Participants and raw response amplitudes were not pooled across modalities, and independent cohorts were not treated as a longitudinal intervention study.

# Methods

## Study design and evidence roles

This study comprises secondary analyses of public EEG data. Analyses frozen before full-cohort computation are distinguished from analyses specified after inspection of published results or released summary tables; none is described as prospectively preregistered. Participants, rather than epochs, trials, runs, or stimulation blocks, are the units of group-level inference unless a model explicitly estimates a within-participant trial effect with participant clustering or fixed effects. Repeated observations do not increase the nominal number of independent participants.

Cohort flow, missingness, and outcome-specific effective sample sizes are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Acquisition states, preprocessing choices, estimands, covariate sets, multiplicity families, and interpretation boundaries are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

The datasets were not pooled at the participant level. Resting EEG from ds004504 and visual photostimulation EEG from ds006036 share participant identifiers and permit within-participant resting-to-visual analyses. The other datasets are independent. ds005048 tests 40 Hz auditory target engagement and MMSE; ds006222 tests signal availability and the consequences of unavailable behavioral files; ds007648 tests trial-level 36/40 Hz state–behavior associations; ds006780 tests developmental sensitivity and adjusted behavioral and cognitive validity; and the Dryad cohort tests mean active-control benefit, between- versus within-participant neural association, and pretreatment prediction of personalized benefit.

## Public resting and visual datasets

OpenNeuro ds004504 contains eyes-closed resting EEG from 88 participants: 36 with AD, 23 with FTD, and 29 cognitively normal controls. Recordings used 19 scalp electrodes at 500 Hz and lasted approximately 12–14 min. MMSE was available, although control participants had a ceiling score of 30. The continuous severity analyses were therefore interpreted within the constraints imposed by this distribution.

OpenNeuro ds006036 contains intermittent visual photostimulation EEG from the same 88 participants, acquired with 19 electrodes at 500 Hz. The main stimulation frequencies were 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz. Event auditing identified 407 nominal frequency blocks and 372 blocks with reconstructable eye-state exposure. Because event durations were stored as zero and stimulation labels usually preceded eye opening, analysis windows were reconstructed from the intersection of the hardware pulse train and eye-state markers (Supplementary Figure S1). For the eyes-open local response analysis, a block required at least 2.5 s of usable exposure; the corresponding usable samples were 80, 76, 78, and 77 participants at 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz, respectively. Sixty-eight participants had usable data at all four frequencies.

## Public auditory dataset

OpenNeuro ds005048 contains 40 Hz auditory stimulation EEG from 35 adults aged 54–89 years, including participants classified as cognitively normal, MCI, mild AD, or moderate AD; two records had no diagnostic label. EEG was recorded from 19 scalp electrodes at 250 Hz. A 5 kHz carrier was amplitude modulated by a 40 Hz rectangular waveform with a 4% duty cycle. Stimulation blocks lasting 40 s alternated with 20-s resting intervals. Some recordings contained six stimulation blocks and others contained ten; analyses that required a common observation schedule used the first six blocks from every participant. MMSE was available for 33 participants. Because several diagnostic labels were discordant with MMSE, continuous MMSE was the principal clinical variable and diagnosis was used only in secondary analyses.

## Public signal-availability and behavioral-validation datasets

OpenNeuro ds006222 version 1.0.1 contains 69 participants and 70 recordings from 40 Hz audiovisual, visual-flicker, random, and light-control conditions. The source article reports 70 enrolled participants and an analyzed sample of 62 after eight behavioral outlier exclusions [L044]. Metadata and signal inspection established that the released EEG could support a target-engagement gate. Trial outcomes and response times, however, were stored by the original task in separate PsychoPy files that were not included in the public release, and the released events did not locate the required occlusion periods. We therefore could not independently reconstruct the article's behavioral contrasts, exclusion rule, or neural–behavioral associations from the public package. The dataset was retained as a data-availability and signal-QC result and was not used as positive or negative efficacy evidence.

OpenNeuro ds007648 version 1.1.0 contains 22 healthy adults performing a cross-modal attention task with simultaneous 36 Hz visual and 40 Hz auditory frequency tags [L037]. Two released recordings were technically unusable for neural analysis: one had frame misalignment and one was truncated relative to its event range. The remaining 20 participants contributed 8,579 trials to the primary neural–accuracy analysis and 7,456 correct trials to the response-time analysis. Technical exclusions were defined without reference to accuracy, response time, or neural effect size.

OpenNeuro ds006780 version 1.0.0 contains 27/40 Hz auditory steady-state oddball recordings from autistic children, typically developing children, and unaffected siblings [L038]. Of 123 participants with successfully derived auditory steady-state records, 111 retained at least 100 valid standard trials at both frequencies and finite neural measures; 109 also had full-scale IQ. Multiple runs were combined within participant using valid-trial weighting before group-level analysis.

## Personalized-theta dataset

The Dryad dataset with DOI 10.5061/dryad.t76hdr8dm contains 44 adults aged 60–75 years who completed a baseline auditory Simon task and four within-participant stimulation conditions: the individualized theta frequency, 1.33 times that frequency, fixed 2 Hz stimulation, and a non-rhythmic condition [L039]. Each condition comprised 96 trials. We first analyzed the complete participant-level table released by the authors. Raw-EEG development used five participants selected without reference to behavioral benefit. The frozen technical protocol was then applied condition-blind to the remaining 39 participants; 35 passed and formed the confirmatory cohort, while four failed prespecified technical criteria. Development participants and technical failures were excluded from all confirmatory estimates.

The released neural field was generated by the authors from a frontocentral narrow-band signal after averaging across correct trials and electrodes before Hilbert transformation. We therefore refer to it as the author-derived evoked narrow-band response change when its measurement semantics are material. The independent raw-EEG analysis used inter-trial phase coherence (ITPC), evoked local log signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), total-power local log SNR, and induced local log SNR as distinct measures. We retain *entrainment* only when naming the released field or the source study's construct; none of these measures alone distinguishes endogenous oscillatory entrainment from repeated or temporally predictable evoked activity.

## EEG quality control and preprocessing

All public recordings were checked for file completeness, channel structure, sampling frequency, duration, event consistency, and finite signal values before feature extraction. Quality-control summaries were retained at the recording and participant levels. Missing or unusable features were retained as missing and were not replaced by zero. Analyses report the number of participants entering each estimate rather than the number originally enrolled in the dataset.

For resting EEG, the candidate feature family comprised individual alpha peak frequency, relative band powers, spectral slowing, and the aperiodic exponent. Features were summarized over a posterior region of interest using available channels among O1, O2, P3, P4, and Pz. A reliable individual alpha peak was searched within 7–13 Hz after accounting for the aperiodic background; recordings without a credible peak were flagged rather than assigned a default frequency.

## Visual response measures

The eyes-open local visual response was summarized at O1 and O2 as the decibel ratio of power at the stimulation frequency to power in adjacent frequency bins. This measure was used to establish local frequency-following detectability and to test whether resting spectral features predicted the visual response. The prespecified occipital summary was checked against descriptive channel-wide distributions (Supplementary Figure S2). Frequencies with sparse or nonstandard coverage were not included in the primary analysis.

The closed-eye analysis reproduced the response definition used in the direct competing analysis [L024]. For each stimulation frequency, a posterior-region driving index was calculated as target-frequency power divided by the median power from a ±2 Hz neighborhood after excluding bins within ±0.5 Hz of the target. The posterior region included O1, O2, P3, P4, Pz, P7, and P8 when available. The primary frequency-selectivity contrast was the 10 Hz driving index minus the mean driving index at 5, 15, and 20 Hz. This closed-eye driving index differs from the eyes-open O1/O2 decibel signal-to-noise ratio in state, region, time window, and denominator; the two values were not combined or treated as interchangeable measures.

## Auditory response measures

The auditory response was summarized over frontocentral electrodes using the change in narrowband 40 Hz signal-to-noise ratio between stimulation and the adjacent resting condition. The primary analysis used the first six common stimulation blocks. Within-recording stability was assessed by correlating participant-level summaries from blocks 1–3 with summaries from blocks 4–6. This coefficient is described as split-half stability, not test–retest reliability, because the two estimates came from the same recording session. Exploratory temporal and non-zero-lag connectivity measures had insufficient reliability or no incremental association with MMSE and were retained only as supplementary measurement-boundary analyses.

## Trial-level 36/40 Hz response measures

For ds007648, the primary trial measure was the complex target-frequency coefficient during the final 0.5 s before target onset, projected onto the participant's mean phase direction estimated from all other trials. The leave-one-trial-out phase reference prevented the current trial from defining its own alignment score. The 36 Hz measure used a fixed occipital region, and the 40 Hz measure used a fixed central region. Each measure was standardized within participant. Odd–even trial inter-trial phase coherence described the stability of participant rankings for the aggregate response; it was not treated as reliability of the within-participant trial-state score. Narrow-band envelope measures were sensitivity analyses.

Accuracy was modeled with participant fixed effects, task-condition terms, trial number, both within-participant neural measures, and their target-modality interactions. Correct-trial log response time used the same fixed structure. Standard errors were clustered by participant. Marginal effects averaged across auditory and visual targets were computed from the fitted contrasts. A stricter ocular-artifact subset, exclusion of the participant with late-recording noise, amplitude-based measures, between-participant correlations, and two-stage participant-specific slopes were examined as sensitivity analyses. Incremental prediction compared task-only and task-plus-neural models with leave-one-participant-out validation; folds never split trials from one participant across training and testing.

## Developmental 27/40 Hz response measures

For ds006780, EEG was re-referenced to the available TP7/TP8 mean. The central region comprised FCz, FC3, and FC4. Epochs extended from −200 ms, and the primary response window was 200–500 ms. Neural summaries included target-frequency local log signal-to-noise ratio, inter-trial phase coherence, and Morlet power relative to a −150 to −50 ms baseline. Odd–even inter-trial phase-coherence coefficients were corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula and are described as within-recording reliability. This reliability estimate was paired only with inter-trial phase-coherence validity models and was not transferred to local signal-to-noise ratio or Morlet power.

Button presses were assigned to the most recent unmatched stimulus within 1 s. Hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections were reconstructed separately for the 27 and 40 Hz standard conditions, and d-prime used a log-linear correction at ceiling or floor. Alternative 0.75- and 1.25-s assignment windows were sensitivity analyses.

To determine whether limited neural–behavioral correspondence could be explained by unreliable outcomes at the same level of analysis, we estimated behavioral split-half reliability in ds007648 and ds006780. Trials were assigned to alternating halves within participant and trial type, preserving the task composition of each half. In ds007648, the half-level outcomes were accuracy and median response time on correct trials, restricted to the EEG-valid analysis set. In ds006780, hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections were reconstructed within each half and then aggregated across runs before calculating corrected d-prime for the 27 and 40 Hz standard conditions. Odd–even rank correlations were corrected to full task length with the Spearman–Brown formula; 95% confidence intervals were obtained by resampling participants 10,000 times. These coefficients quantify within-session stability of between-participant rankings and do not estimate within-participant trial fluctuations or between-day repeatability.

The primary 40 Hz behavioral model adjusted for the corresponding 27 Hz measure, age, sex, and diagnostic group and used HC3 standard errors. Full-scale IQ used the same adjustment set. Secondary models assessed quadratic age, group interactions, alternative neural metrics, signal quality, influence, and eligibility ranges from the source study. Repeated 10×10-fold cross-validation compared covariate-only models with models that added the neural features. Folds were defined at the participant level.

## Personalized-theta estimands

Paired contrasts compared each personalized condition and their mean with fixed 2 Hz stimulation. Response time was the primary behavioral measure; accuracy and inverse efficiency defined the speed–accuracy boundary. The difference between 1.33-times-individualized and individualized theta tested whether the faster personalized rhythm added benefit.

The released neural response was decomposed into each participant's mean across rhythmic conditions and the condition-specific deviation from that mean. Post-stimulation response time and inverse efficiency were modeled with condition, baseline response time, age, sex, and both neural components; standard errors were clustered by participant. A participant-fixed-effect model and 20,000 within-participant permutations tested the condition-level component without relying on between-participant differences. Baseline-performance moderation was calculated separately for improvement from baseline, personalized advantage over the non-rhythmic condition, and personalized advantage over active 2 Hz stimulation.

Pretreatment prediction used nested leave-one-participant-out ridge regression with individualized theta frequency, baseline response time, age, and sex. Model mean absolute and root mean squared errors were compared with a training-set-mean benchmark. Hyperparameter selection was confined to each outer training set.

The separately frozen raw-EEG analysis compared the theta-plus condition (1.33 times the individual theta frequency) with the non-rhythmic condition at their common analysis frequency. Data were common-average referenced across frozen-QC good scalp channels, resampled by a factor of four, and band-pass filtered from 1 to 45 Hz. Seven-second epochs spanned −3 to 4 s around stimulation onset. Target-frequency phase was estimated after filtering within ±1 Hz on the full epoch, and the fixed analysis window was 0.25–1.75 s. The frontocentral region comprised FC3, FC1, FCz, FC2, and FC4, with at least three good channels required and no bad-channel interpolation.

The single primary neural estimand was the paired participant mean difference in frontocentral ITPC between theta-plus and non-rhythmic stimulation. A two-sided 95% *t* interval and participant-bootstrap sensitivity were accompanied by an operational equivalence analysis using a 90% interval and ±0.30 paired-SD bounds. The three key secondary neural contrasts—evoked, total-power, and induced local log SNR—formed a Holm-corrected family. Internal consistency of ITPC and evoked local log SNR was estimated separately by odd–even and time-half splits within condition, using Spearman correlations corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula and participant-bootstrap intervals; these estimates are not between-day test–retest reliability.

Behavioral contrasts compared theta-plus with non-rhythmic stimulation for accuracy, median correct response time, and inverse efficiency and were Holm corrected. For condition-specific concurrent coupling, a leave-one-trial-out phase-alignment score was centered within participant and condition. Correctness was modeled with binomial generalized estimating equations and correct-trial log response time with Gaussian generalized estimating equations, both clustered by participant and including a condition interaction. The behavioral denominator included all event-valid trials, whereas coupling analyses additionally required the frozen EEG artifact rules. These concurrent models were not interpreted as mediation or causal ordering.

Incremental prediction targeted each participant's theta-plus-minus-non-rhythmic inverse-efficiency contrast. Nested leave-one-participant-out ridge regression compared a baseline-feature model (individual theta frequency, baseline response time, age, and sex) with a model adding the ITPC contrast, evoked-SNR contrast, and internal consistency. Prediction error was evaluated against both the baseline-feature model and the training-fold mean using mean absolute error, root mean squared error, and participant-bootstrap uncertainty. The prediction gate required improvement over both comparators with uncertainty excluding no improvement; exploratory prediction could not rescue a failed primary or coupling result.

## Statistical analysis

Resting EEG associations were estimated at the participant level. In ds004504, associations between spectral features and MMSE established the public reference pattern. Diagnostic-stage summaries were secondary to continuous associations because controls had an MMSE ceiling.

For the visual data, detectability was summarized by participant-level response distributions at each frequency. Diagnostic contrasts in the raw 10 Hz driving index used heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors. Because the distribution was right-skewed, the raw estimate was reported alongside prespecified log1p and 5th/95th-percentile winsorized sensitivity analyses. Frequency selectivity was tested with the within-participant 10 Hz-minus-other-frequencies contrast and checked against a repeated-frequency model. Associations with MMSE were estimated separately within AD and FTD to avoid treating the control-group ceiling as continuous severity information.

Resting-to-visual correspondence was tested in the participants shared by ds004504 and ds006036. Seven resting features were related to the eyes-open local response at four frequencies, with age, sex, and diagnosis included as covariates. The resulting 28 tests were controlled as one false-discovery-rate family. These models apply only to the defined eyes-open O1/O2 response and do not test all possible visual-response measures.

For the auditory data, target engagement was summarized by the participant-level stimulation-minus-resting 40 Hz response and the proportion of positive individual effects. Split-half stability and the monotonic association with MMSE were quantified with Spearman correlations. Network and temporal analyses were treated as exploratory and remained in the supplementary material after they failed the prespecified reliability or clinical-increment gates. Response-scale, reference and leave-one-subject-out checks are shown in Supplementary Figure S3, and all advanced multiplicity families are shown in Supplementary Figure S4.

All main results include the effective sample size, effect estimate, 95% confidence interval, and relevant model diagnostics. False-discovery-rate correction was retained for predefined feature families. Null-hypothesis tests were not interpreted as proof of no association. Where sensitivity bounds rather than established clinical minimum differences were available, equivalence analyses were labeled as design-sensitivity analyses rather than clinical equivalence tests. For ds006780, d-prime was standardized by its standard deviation in the technical sample, and symmetric bounds of 0.20, 0.25, and 0.30 outcome SD were evaluated separately for local log signal-to-noise ratio and inter-trial phase coherence. The two predictors were not pooled because only the inter-trial phase-coherence model was aligned with the corresponding neural reliability estimate.

Complete visual models, auditory models, and resting-to-visual models are reported in Supplementary Tables S3, S4, and S5, respectively. Blank multiplicity-adjusted cells indicate that an adjustment was not defined for that standalone analysis; they are not treated as zero.

## Reproducibility and reporting

Public-data analyses were implemented as executable scripts and notebooks with dataset-level manifests, participant-flow outputs, machine-readable model tables, and input checksums. The direct 10 Hz analysis is labeled a computational replication of a same-data preprint, not an external replication. For Dryad, the technical inclusion rules, participant set, primary and secondary estimands, multiplicity families, sensitivity analyses, result branches, and claim ceiling were frozen before confirmatory signal re-access. All 35 participant derivations were completed before the locked group pipeline was run once. This is a frozen secondary analysis, not a prospectively registered experiment. A local release candidate passed independent derived-output reproduction and a machine screen for raw EEG, identifiers, credentials, and absolute paths; public archiving remains contingent on final licensing and author approval.
