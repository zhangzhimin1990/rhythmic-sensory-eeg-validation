# 4. Discussion

Across seven public human EEG datasets, rhythmic sensory responses were usually detectable and often stable within a recording, but stronger interpretations required separate tests. Four recurrent breaks appeared in the evidential chain: detectability or group sensitivity did not establish a state-general mechanism; within-recording reliability did not establish matched cognitive validity; a mean active-control behavioral advantage did not establish within-participant neural–behavioral coupling; and an in-sample association did not establish incremental prediction for new participants. These are bounded counterexamples to automatic claim upgrading, not evidence that rhythmic stimulation lacks physiological or behavioral effects.

The visual findings show why a group contrast is insufficient for individual biomarker qualification. Closed-eye 10-Hz selectivity differed by diagnostic group, yet the raw-scale interval was imprecise, the reported split-half intraclass correlation was 0.06, and within-AD and within-FTD MMSE associations were weak and directionally inconsistent. The eyes-open O1/O2 response did not reproduce that diagnostic pattern. Because the analyses differed simultaneously in eye state, interval, region, scale, and denominator, the contrast cannot be attributed to eye closure alone. It instead establishes that state and response definition must be fixed before a stimulation marker is evaluated. A stimulus-frequency peak alone remains compatible with repeated evoked activity or temporal predictability rather than endogenous-oscillator entrainment [@L001_Duecker2024; @L007_Duecker2021; @L041_Breska2017].

The linked resting-to-stimulation analysis further separated disease-associated spontaneous activity from the measured visual response. Resting slowing, alpha features, and spectral exponent tracked MMSE, but did not predict the defined eyes-open response after adjustment and multiplicity control. This does not establish biological independence; it shows that a disease-related resting feature cannot be assumed to predict a stimulation response without a direct, prespecified test.

The auditory cohorts provided complementary boundaries. Older-adult 40-Hz responses were stable within recordings, yet response magnitude, spatial gradient, imaginary coherence, and adaptation did not add MMSE information. In the developmental cohort, reliable 40-Hz inter-trial phase coherence and d-prime coexisted with an imprecise adjusted association. In the cross-modal attention cohort, aggregate 40-Hz phase coherence was reliable but showed near-zero aggregate behavioral associations; a separate trial-state association was interpreted only at that within-participant level. Absolute amplitudes cannot be compared across cohorts because participants, stimuli, systems, references, and definitions differed. Their shared contribution is narrower: reliability did not guarantee concurrent or predictive validity when the metric and aggregation level were matched.

The published ds006222 source study provides complementary positive evidence: its 40-Hz audiovisual group showed better vigilance accuracy and response time than constant-light and random-flicker groups, with exploratory associations involving delta power and lower-alpha connectivity [@L044_Attokaren2026]. The public release available here omitted the separate PsychoPy outcomes required to reconstruct those behavioral exclusions and contrasts. We therefore did not recode unavailable endpoints as null findings or present the published associations as independently reproduced. This mismatch defines a reproducibility boundary: open EEG can permit target-engagement checks while remaining insufficient for independent qualification of the linked behavioral endpoint.

The frozen personalized-theta analysis showed positive neural and behavioral effects within the same cohort. Temporal regularity produced a large frontocentral phase-locking difference, a selectively evoked spectral response, and faster performance with improved inverse efficiency. Nevertheless, trial-to-trial phase alignment did not track correctness or response time within either condition, and participant-level neural features did not consistently improve held-out prediction. Target engagement, acute performance, concurrent coupling, and prediction therefore separated in the same participants. The absence of supported coupling does not negate the neural or behavioral effects; it limits the claim that the measured phase response was their condition-specific behavioral mechanism.

This result empirically complements the distinction between physiological resonance and artificial superimposition [@L043_Qi2026]. The selective evoked response is compatible with phase-consistent stimulus following, but the design cannot establish endogenous resonance, deep-brain engagement, or the proposed biological effects of repeated 40-Hz treatment. Mechanism specificity and cognitive-marker validity are related but non-interchangeable requirements. Comparator choice likewise mattered: slower baseline performance related to change from baseline and advantage over a non-rhythmic condition, but not to added benefit over active 2-Hz stimulation. An average active-control effect did not yield a validated rule for selecting stimulation for a new participant.

Therapeutic interpretation remains correspondingly limited. OVERTURE produced EEG-verified target engagement without separation on its principal cognitive outcomes, although a selected imaging subset produced structural signals [@L020_L027_Hajos2024; @L035_Da2024]. The same endpoint-level tension appeared in meta-analysis [@L013_Ang2025]. These results cannot qualify EEG synchronization as a surrogate. The HOPE pivotal trial had completed follow-up but had not posted results at the literature cutoff [@L036_HOPE], and a randomized 40-Hz versus non-40-Hz music trial with EEG primary outcomes remained in recruitment [@L042_GammaAuditoryTrial]. The present datasets include acute behavior but no longitudinal clinical outcome. They support requirements for future qualification—state-robust engagement, between-day reliability, prespecified within-participant clinical coupling, and external prediction—rather than a chronic-efficacy conclusion.

The cohorts do not represent a single disease continuum. AD and FTD share cognitive impairment and resting spectral slowing but differ in pathology, topography, and clinical phenotype; cognitively normal older adults additionally differ from patients in disease burden and cognitive range. We therefore confined disease-related estimates to linked AD, FTD, and control recordings and used independent healthy-aging and developmental cohorts only to test measurement properties and inferential transitions. Cross-cohort convergence concerns claim qualification, not a pooled biological effect. This design respects heterogeneity but cannot establish that the same transition failures have identical causes across populations.

Event timing was reconstructed from hardware and eye-state markers; technical exclusions were separated from behavioral outcomes; and response-scale, reference, artifact, eligibility-range, comparator, complete-case, and behavioral-reliability sensitivities were retained. Group effects, within-group severity, stability, concurrent association, active-control benefit, coupling, and prediction were treated as distinct estimands. The 35-participant raw-EEG confirmation was frozen before condition-level results were accessed, and its evoked, total, and induced measures were separated.

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. These are secondary analyses; only the Dryad raw-EEG confirmation was frozen before confirmatory signal re-access. MMSE is coarse and showed ceiling effects or diagnostic discordance in some cohorts. Split-half coefficients do not replace between-day reliability. Trial-level associations remain vulnerable to time-varying common causes, and null intervals do not prove that every neural–behavioral relationship is absent. The acute personalized-theta cohort comprised healthy older adults rather than patients with cognitive impairment. The data do not support anatomical source, directed propagation, endogenous entrainment, chronic efficacy, disease modification, or patient treatment selection.

Human rhythmic-sensory EEG responses were measurable, but their meaning depended on state, metric, comparator, population, and claim. A diagnostic-group contrast could coexist with low individual reliability; a reliable response could lack cognitive validity; and an average active-control benefit could coexist with unsupported condition-level coupling and individual prediction. Sensory target engagement is therefore an initial observation, not by itself a cognitive biomarker, treatment-selection marker, or clinical surrogate.
