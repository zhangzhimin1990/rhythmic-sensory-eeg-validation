# 1. Introduction

Rhythmic sensory stimulation can elicit large stimulus-locked electroencephalographic responses without invasive intervention. This property has motivated its use in Alzheimer's disease (AD), particularly at 40 Hz, as both a physiological probe and a candidate therapeutic approach. Human visual, auditory, and audiovisual studies have confirmed responses at the driving frequency in healthy adults and people with cognitive impairment [@L003_Chan2022; @L014_Lahijanian2024; @L020_L027_Hajos2024]. A stimulus-frequency peak, however, establishes that the recorded signal follows the stimulus; it does not by itself show that an endogenous oscillator has been entrained. Visually driven and endogenous gamma responses can arise from distinct, coexisting generators, while repeated evoked responses can produce apparent phase locking [@L001_Duecker2024; @L007_Duecker2021]. We therefore use *frequency-following response* or *target engagement* for the measured EEG effect and reserve *entrainment* for designs that test stronger dynamical criteria.

This distinction matters clinically because target engagement, diagnostic-group sensitivity, individual cognitive validity, treatment selection, and surrogate qualification require different evidence. A recent synthesis framed physiological resonance versus artificial superimposition as a central unresolved determinant of deep-brain engagement and therapeutic outcome [@L043_Qi2026]. Operationally, however, a reliable response may still show weak cognitive validity, a mean behavioral effect may occur without within-participant neural–behavioral coupling, and an in-sample association may fail to improve prediction in new participants. Treating these findings as one construct can overstate what an online EEG response establishes.

Human findings illustrate the problem. Forty-hertz auditory steady-state responses have shown good long-term reliability in healthy participants [@L032_Hirano2020], while case-control studies have reported both enhanced auditory response power in AD and attenuated stimulus-induced gamma in mild cognitive impairment or AD [@L016_vanDeursen2011; @L017_Murty2021]. A public photostimulation analysis reported selective 10-Hz attenuation in AD and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), but the same measure had near-null split-half reliability and did not improve classification beyond resting EEG [@L024_VelezPardo2026]. Small feasibility studies have also reported online gamma responses and exploratory benefits [@L003_Chan2022; @L026_Agger2023]. In the six-month randomized OVERTURE study, however, EEG-verified target engagement did not accompany separation on the principal cognitive outcomes [@L020_L027_Hajos2024]. A meta-analysis similarly found a structural signal without clear pooled benefit for cognition or activities of daily living [@L013_Ang2025]. These results concern different modalities, states, metrics, and endpoint levels and cannot be reduced to a single positive or negative verdict.

A fixed 40-Hz stimulus adds heterogeneity. In cognitively normal older adults, individual visual center frequencies often fell below 40 Hz [@L031_Yoon2025]. An uncontrolled 16-participant early-AD pilot subsequently linked change in personalized gamma center frequency to cognitive preservation [@L040_Park2026]. This motivates individualized stimulation, but does not yet validate a responder rule. State, waveform, reference, stimulation duration, region of interest, and response denominator may also change the measured effect.

We therefore tested four inferential transitions across seven public human EEG datasets: (1) detectable or group-sensitive response to a state-general mechanism; (2) within-recording reliability to matched cognitive validity; (3) mean active-control behavioral benefit to within-participant neural–behavioral coupling; and (4) in-sample association to held-out incremental prediction. Linked AD, FTD, and control recordings supplied disease-related resting and visual phenotypes. Independent auditory, audiovisual, developmental, and older-adult cohorts tested reliability, cognitive validity, acute behavior, and prediction. A condition-blind frozen raw-EEG analysis in 35 older adults provided the same-cohort test of target engagement, active-control performance, coupling, and individual prediction. Participants and amplitudes were not pooled across cohorts, and the study was not treated as evidence of longitudinal treatment efficacy.
