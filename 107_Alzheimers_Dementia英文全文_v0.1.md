# Abstract

**INTRODUCTION:** Rhythmic sensory stimulation elicits stimulus-locked electroencephalographic responses, but whether these responses qualify as cognitive or clinical markers remains uncertain. We tested four transitions from target engagement to stronger inference.

**METHODS:** We analyzed seven public human EEG datasets spanning cognitive impairment, aging, and development. We separated detectability, robustness, within-recording reliability, cognitive validity, neural–behavioral coupling, and held-out prediction. Raw EEG from 35 older adults was analyzed under a condition-blind frozen protocol.

**RESULTS:** Closed-eye 10-Hz selectivity differed by diagnosis but had low split-half reliability and imprecise within-diagnosis cognitive associations. In older adults, the 40-Hz auditory response was stable (0.838), whereas its Mini-Mental State Examination association was 0.085 (95% CI, −0.283 to 0.417). In the frozen cohort, personalized theta versus non-rhythmic stimulation increased frontocentral inter-trial phase coherence by 0.088 (95% CI, 0.051 to 0.124) and evoked local signal-to-noise ratio by 7.52 dB (95% CI, 4.52 to 10.51). Median response time decreased by 27.73 ms (95% CI, −36.17 to −19.28), but condition-specific neural–behavioral coupling and consistent incremental prediction were unsupported.

**DISCUSSION:** Rhythmic-sensory EEG responses supported target engagement and an acute performance advantage, but not a condition-specific cognitive mechanism proxy. Reliability, group sensitivity, cognitive validity, coupling, and prediction require separate qualification before an EEG response can support biomarker, treatment-selection, or surrogate-endpoint claims.

**TRIAL REGISTRATION:** Not applicable; no participants were enrolled or treated.

**Keywords:** Alzheimer's disease; cognitive impairment; electroencephalography; rhythmic sensory stimulation; frequency-following response; target engagement; cognitive validity; biomarker validation

# Research in Context

**1. Systematic review:** We searched PubMed, bibliographic databases, trial registries, and public-data records for human rhythmic visual, auditory, and audiovisual stimulation studies reporting EEG, cognition, reliability, or prediction. Prior work showed robust stimulus-frequency responses but heterogeneous mechanistic and cognitive findings.

**2. Interpretation:** Across seven public cohorts, we found that target engagement, within-recording reliability, diagnostic sensitivity, cognitive validity, neural–behavioral coupling, and held-out prediction were not interchangeable. Frozen raw-EEG confirmation showed both target engagement and an acute performance advantage without validating the measured phase response as their condition-specific behavioral mechanism.

**3. Future directions:** Prospective patient studies should prespecify state, metric, active comparator, between-day reliability, within-participant clinical coupling, and external prediction before qualifying rhythmic-sensory EEG as a treatment-selection marker or surrogate endpoint.

# 1. Introduction

Rhythmic sensory stimulation can elicit large stimulus-locked electroencephalographic responses without invasive intervention. This property has motivated its use in Alzheimer's disease (AD), particularly at 40 Hz, as both a physiological probe and a candidate therapeutic approach. Human visual, auditory, and audiovisual studies have confirmed responses at the driving frequency in healthy adults and people with cognitive impairment [@L003_Chan2022; @L014_Lahijanian2024; @L020_L027_Hajos2024]. A stimulus-frequency peak, however, establishes that the recorded signal follows the stimulus; it does not by itself show that an endogenous oscillator has been entrained. Visually driven and endogenous gamma responses can arise from distinct, coexisting generators, while repeated evoked responses can produce apparent phase locking [@L001_Duecker2024; @L007_Duecker2021]. We therefore use *frequency-following response* or *target engagement* for the measured EEG effect and reserve *entrainment* for designs that test stronger dynamical criteria.

This distinction matters clinically because target engagement, diagnostic-group sensitivity, individual cognitive validity, treatment selection, and surrogate qualification require different evidence. A recent synthesis framed physiological resonance versus artificial superimposition as a central unresolved determinant of deep-brain engagement and therapeutic outcome [@L043_Qi2026]. Operationally, however, a reliable response may still show weak cognitive validity, a mean behavioral effect may occur without within-participant neural–behavioral coupling, and an in-sample association may fail to improve prediction in new participants. Treating these findings as one construct can overstate what an online EEG response establishes.

Human findings illustrate the problem. Forty-hertz auditory steady-state responses have shown good long-term reliability in healthy participants [@L032_Hirano2020], while case-control studies have reported both enhanced auditory response power in AD and attenuated stimulus-induced gamma in mild cognitive impairment or AD [@L016_vanDeursen2011; @L017_Murty2021]. A public photostimulation analysis reported selective 10-Hz attenuation in AD and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), but the same measure had near-null split-half reliability and did not improve classification beyond resting EEG [@L024_VelezPardo2026]. Small feasibility studies have also reported online gamma responses and exploratory benefits [@L003_Chan2022; @L026_Agger2023]. In the six-month randomized OVERTURE study, however, EEG-verified target engagement did not accompany separation on the principal cognitive outcomes [@L020_L027_Hajos2024]. A meta-analysis similarly found a structural signal without clear pooled benefit for cognition or activities of daily living [@L013_Ang2025]. These results concern different modalities, states, metrics, and endpoint levels and cannot be reduced to a single positive or negative verdict.

A fixed 40-Hz stimulus adds heterogeneity. In cognitively normal older adults, individual visual center frequencies often fell below 40 Hz [@L031_Yoon2025]. An uncontrolled 16-participant early-AD pilot subsequently linked change in personalized gamma center frequency to cognitive preservation [@L040_Park2026]. This motivates individualized stimulation, but does not yet validate a responder rule. State, waveform, reference, stimulation duration, region of interest, and response denominator may also change the measured effect.

We therefore tested four inferential transitions across seven public human EEG datasets: (1) detectable or group-sensitive response to a state-general mechanism; (2) within-recording reliability to matched cognitive validity; (3) mean active-control behavioral benefit to within-participant neural–behavioral coupling; and (4) in-sample association to held-out incremental prediction. Linked AD, FTD, and control recordings supplied disease-related resting and visual phenotypes. Independent auditory, audiovisual, developmental, and older-adult cohorts tested reliability, cognitive validity, acute behavior, and prediction. A condition-blind frozen raw-EEG analysis in 35 older adults provided the same-cohort test of target engagement, active-control performance, coupling, and individual prediction. Participants and amplitudes were not pooled across cohorts, and the study was not treated as evidence of longitudinal treatment efficacy.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Study design and evidence roles

This study comprises secondary analyses of public EEG data. Analyses frozen before full-cohort computation are distinguished from analyses specified after inspection of published results or released summary tables; none is described as prospectively preregistered. Participants, rather than epochs, trials, runs, or stimulation blocks, are the units of group-level inference unless a model explicitly estimates a within-participant trial effect with participant clustering or fixed effects. Repeated observations do not increase the nominal number of independent participants.

Cohort flow, missingness, and outcome-specific effective sample sizes are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Acquisition states, preprocessing choices, estimands, covariate sets, multiplicity families, and interpretation boundaries are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

The datasets were not pooled at the participant level. Resting EEG from ds004504 and visual photostimulation EEG from ds006036 share participant identifiers and permit within-participant resting-to-visual analyses. The other datasets are independent. ds005048 tests 40 Hz auditory target engagement and MMSE; ds006222 tests signal availability and the consequences of unavailable behavioral files; ds007648 tests trial-level 36/40 Hz state–behavior associations; ds006780 tests developmental sensitivity and adjusted behavioral and cognitive validity; and the Dryad cohort tests mean active-control benefit, between- versus within-participant neural association, and pretreatment prediction of personalized benefit.

## 2.2 Public resting and visual datasets

OpenNeuro ds004504 contains eyes-closed resting EEG from 88 participants: 36 with AD, 23 with FTD, and 29 cognitively normal controls. Recordings used 19 scalp electrodes at 500 Hz and lasted approximately 12–14 min. MMSE was available, although control participants had a ceiling score of 30. The continuous severity analyses were therefore interpreted within the constraints imposed by this distribution.

OpenNeuro ds006036 contains intermittent visual photostimulation EEG from the same 88 participants, acquired with 19 electrodes at 500 Hz. The main stimulation frequencies were 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz. Event auditing identified 407 nominal frequency blocks and 372 blocks with reconstructable eye-state exposure. Because event durations were stored as zero and stimulation labels usually preceded eye opening, analysis windows were reconstructed from the intersection of the hardware pulse train and eye-state markers (Supplementary Figure S1). For the eyes-open local response analysis, a block required at least 2.5 s of usable exposure; the corresponding usable samples were 80, 76, 78, and 77 participants at 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz, respectively. Sixty-eight participants had usable data at all four frequencies.

## 2.3 Public auditory dataset

OpenNeuro ds005048 contains 40 Hz auditory stimulation EEG from 35 adults aged 54–89 years, including participants classified as cognitively normal, MCI, mild AD, or moderate AD; two records had no diagnostic label. EEG was recorded from 19 scalp electrodes at 250 Hz. A 5 kHz carrier was amplitude modulated by a 40 Hz rectangular waveform with a 4% duty cycle. Stimulation blocks lasting 40 s alternated with 20-s resting intervals. Some recordings contained six stimulation blocks and others contained ten; analyses that required a common observation schedule used the first six blocks from every participant. MMSE was available for 33 participants. Because several diagnostic labels were discordant with MMSE, continuous MMSE was the principal clinical variable and diagnosis was used only in secondary analyses.

## 2.4 Public signal-availability and behavioral-validation datasets

OpenNeuro ds006222 version 1.0.1 contains 69 participants and 70 recordings from 40 Hz audiovisual, visual-flicker, random, and light-control conditions. The source article reports 70 enrolled participants and an analyzed sample of 62 after eight behavioral outlier exclusions [@L044_Attokaren2026]. Metadata and signal inspection established that the released EEG could support a target-engagement gate. Trial outcomes and response times, however, were stored by the original task in separate PsychoPy files that were not included in the public release, and the released events did not locate the required occlusion periods. We therefore could not independently reconstruct the article's behavioral contrasts, exclusion rule, or neural–behavioral associations from the public package. The dataset was retained as a data-availability and signal-QC result and was not used as positive or negative efficacy evidence.

OpenNeuro ds007648 version 1.1.0 contains 22 healthy adults performing a cross-modal attention task with simultaneous 36 Hz visual and 40 Hz auditory frequency tags [@L037_Brickwedde2025]. Two released recordings were technically unusable for neural analysis: one had frame misalignment and one was truncated relative to its event range. The remaining 20 participants contributed 8,579 trials to the primary neural–accuracy analysis and 7,456 correct trials to the response-time analysis. Technical exclusions were defined without reference to accuracy, response time, or neural effect size.

OpenNeuro ds006780 version 1.0.0 contains 27/40 Hz auditory steady-state oddball recordings from autistic children, typically developing children, and unaffected siblings [@L038_Darrell2026]. Of 123 participants with successfully derived auditory steady-state records, 111 retained at least 100 valid standard trials at both frequencies and finite neural measures; 109 also had full-scale IQ. Multiple runs were combined within participant using valid-trial weighting before group-level analysis.

## 2.5 Personalized-theta dataset

The Dryad dataset with DOI 10.5061/dryad.t76hdr8dm contains 44 adults aged 60–75 years who completed a baseline auditory Simon task and four within-participant stimulation conditions: the individualized theta frequency, 1.33 times that frequency, fixed 2 Hz stimulation, and a non-rhythmic condition [@L039_GomezLombardi2026]. Each condition comprised 96 trials. We first analyzed the complete participant-level table released by the authors. Raw-EEG development used five participants selected without reference to behavioral benefit. The frozen technical protocol was then applied condition-blind to the remaining 39 participants; 35 passed and formed the confirmatory cohort, while four failed prespecified technical criteria. Development participants and technical failures were excluded from all confirmatory estimates.

The released neural field was generated by the authors from a frontocentral narrow-band signal after averaging across correct trials and electrodes before Hilbert transformation. We therefore refer to it as the author-derived evoked narrow-band response change when its measurement semantics are material. The independent raw-EEG analysis used inter-trial phase coherence (ITPC), evoked local log signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), total-power local log SNR, and induced local log SNR as distinct measures. We retain *entrainment* only when naming the released field or the source study's construct; none of these measures alone distinguishes endogenous oscillatory entrainment from repeated or temporally predictable evoked activity.

## 2.6 EEG quality control and preprocessing

All public recordings were checked for file completeness, channel structure, sampling frequency, duration, event consistency, and finite signal values before feature extraction. Quality-control summaries were retained at the recording and participant levels. Missing or unusable features were retained as missing and were not replaced by zero. Analyses report the number of participants entering each estimate rather than the number originally enrolled in the dataset.

For resting EEG, the candidate feature family comprised individual alpha peak frequency, relative band powers, spectral slowing, and the aperiodic exponent. Features were summarized over a posterior region of interest using available channels among O1, O2, P3, P4, and Pz. A reliable individual alpha peak was searched within 7–13 Hz after accounting for the aperiodic background; recordings without a credible peak were flagged rather than assigned a default frequency.

## 2.7 Visual response measures

The eyes-open local visual response was summarized at O1 and O2 as the decibel ratio of power at the stimulation frequency to power in adjacent frequency bins. This measure was used to establish local frequency-following detectability and to test whether resting spectral features predicted the visual response. The prespecified occipital summary was checked against descriptive channel-wide distributions (Supplementary Figure S2). Frequencies with sparse or nonstandard coverage were not included in the primary analysis.

The closed-eye analysis reproduced the response definition used in the direct competing analysis [@L024_VelezPardo2026]. For each stimulation frequency, a posterior-region driving index was calculated as target-frequency power divided by the median power from a ±2 Hz neighborhood after excluding bins within ±0.5 Hz of the target. The posterior region included O1, O2, P3, P4, Pz, P7, and P8 when available. The primary frequency-selectivity contrast was the 10 Hz driving index minus the mean driving index at 5, 15, and 20 Hz. This closed-eye driving index differs from the eyes-open O1/O2 decibel signal-to-noise ratio in state, region, time window, and denominator; the two values were not combined or treated as interchangeable measures.

## 2.8 Auditory response measures

The auditory response was summarized over frontocentral electrodes using the change in narrowband 40 Hz signal-to-noise ratio between stimulation and the adjacent resting condition. The primary analysis used the first six common stimulation blocks. Within-recording stability was assessed by correlating participant-level summaries from blocks 1–3 with summaries from blocks 4–6. This coefficient is described as split-half stability, not test–retest reliability, because the two estimates came from the same recording session. Exploratory temporal and non-zero-lag connectivity measures had insufficient reliability or no incremental association with MMSE and were retained only as supplementary measurement-boundary analyses.

## 2.9 Trial-level 36/40 Hz response measures

For ds007648, the primary trial measure was the complex target-frequency coefficient during the final 0.5 s before target onset, projected onto the participant's mean phase direction estimated from all other trials. The leave-one-trial-out phase reference prevented the current trial from defining its own alignment score. The 36 Hz measure used a fixed occipital region, and the 40 Hz measure used a fixed central region. Each measure was standardized within participant. Odd–even trial inter-trial phase coherence described the stability of participant rankings for the aggregate response; it was not treated as reliability of the within-participant trial-state score. Narrow-band envelope measures were sensitivity analyses.

Accuracy was modeled with participant fixed effects, task-condition terms, trial number, both within-participant neural measures, and their target-modality interactions. Correct-trial log response time used the same fixed structure. Standard errors were clustered by participant. Marginal effects averaged across auditory and visual targets were computed from the fitted contrasts. A stricter ocular-artifact subset, exclusion of the participant with late-recording noise, amplitude-based measures, between-participant correlations, and two-stage participant-specific slopes were examined as sensitivity analyses. Incremental prediction compared task-only and task-plus-neural models with leave-one-participant-out validation; folds never split trials from one participant across training and testing.

## 2.10 Developmental 27/40 Hz response measures

For ds006780, EEG was re-referenced to the available TP7/TP8 mean. The central region comprised FCz, FC3, and FC4. Epochs extended from −200 ms, and the primary response window was 200–500 ms. Neural summaries included target-frequency local log signal-to-noise ratio, inter-trial phase coherence, and Morlet power relative to a −150 to −50 ms baseline. Odd–even inter-trial phase-coherence coefficients were corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula and are described as within-recording reliability. This reliability estimate was paired only with inter-trial phase-coherence validity models and was not transferred to local signal-to-noise ratio or Morlet power.

Button presses were assigned to the most recent unmatched stimulus within 1 s. Hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections were reconstructed separately for the 27 and 40 Hz standard conditions, and d-prime used a log-linear correction at ceiling or floor. Alternative 0.75- and 1.25-s assignment windows were sensitivity analyses.

To determine whether limited neural–behavioral correspondence could be explained by unreliable outcomes at the same level of analysis, we estimated behavioral split-half reliability in ds007648 and ds006780. Trials were assigned to alternating halves within participant and trial type, preserving the task composition of each half. In ds007648, the half-level outcomes were accuracy and median response time on correct trials, restricted to the EEG-valid analysis set. In ds006780, hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections were reconstructed within each half and then aggregated across runs before calculating corrected d-prime for the 27 and 40 Hz standard conditions. Odd–even rank correlations were corrected to full task length with the Spearman–Brown formula; 95% confidence intervals were obtained by resampling participants 10,000 times. These coefficients quantify within-session stability of between-participant rankings and do not estimate within-participant trial fluctuations or between-day repeatability.

The primary 40 Hz behavioral model adjusted for the corresponding 27 Hz measure, age, sex, and diagnostic group and used HC3 standard errors. Full-scale IQ used the same adjustment set. Secondary models assessed quadratic age, group interactions, alternative neural metrics, signal quality, influence, and eligibility ranges from the source study. Repeated 10×10-fold cross-validation compared covariate-only models with models that added the neural features. Folds were defined at the participant level.

## 2.11 Personalized-theta estimands

Paired contrasts compared each personalized condition and their mean with fixed 2 Hz stimulation. Response time was the primary behavioral measure; accuracy and inverse efficiency defined the speed–accuracy boundary. The difference between 1.33-times-individualized and individualized theta tested whether the faster personalized rhythm added benefit.

The released neural response was decomposed into each participant's mean across rhythmic conditions and the condition-specific deviation from that mean. Post-stimulation response time and inverse efficiency were modeled with condition, baseline response time, age, sex, and both neural components; standard errors were clustered by participant. A participant-fixed-effect model and 20,000 within-participant permutations tested the condition-level component without relying on between-participant differences. Baseline-performance moderation was calculated separately for improvement from baseline, personalized advantage over the non-rhythmic condition, and personalized advantage over active 2 Hz stimulation.

Pretreatment prediction used nested leave-one-participant-out ridge regression with individualized theta frequency, baseline response time, age, and sex. Model mean absolute and root mean squared errors were compared with a training-set-mean benchmark. Hyperparameter selection was confined to each outer training set.

The separately frozen raw-EEG analysis compared the theta-plus condition (1.33 times the individual theta frequency) with the non-rhythmic condition at their common analysis frequency. Data were common-average referenced across frozen-QC good scalp channels, resampled by a factor of four, and band-pass filtered from 1 to 45 Hz. Seven-second epochs spanned −3 to 4 s around stimulation onset. Target-frequency phase was estimated after filtering within ±1 Hz on the full epoch, and the fixed analysis window was 0.25–1.75 s. The frontocentral region comprised FC3, FC1, FCz, FC2, and FC4, with at least three good channels required and no bad-channel interpolation.

The single primary neural estimand was the paired participant mean difference in frontocentral ITPC between theta-plus and non-rhythmic stimulation. A two-sided 95% *t* interval and participant-bootstrap sensitivity were accompanied by an operational equivalence analysis using a 90% interval and ±0.30 paired-SD bounds. The three key secondary neural contrasts—evoked, total-power, and induced local log SNR—formed a Holm-corrected family. Internal consistency of ITPC and evoked local log SNR was estimated separately by odd–even and time-half splits within condition, using Spearman correlations corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula and participant-bootstrap intervals; these estimates are not between-day test–retest reliability.

Behavioral contrasts compared theta-plus with non-rhythmic stimulation for accuracy, median correct response time, and inverse efficiency and were Holm corrected. For condition-specific concurrent coupling, a leave-one-trial-out phase-alignment score was centered within participant and condition. Correctness was modeled with binomial generalized estimating equations and correct-trial log response time with Gaussian generalized estimating equations, both clustered by participant and including a condition interaction. The behavioral denominator included all event-valid trials, whereas coupling analyses additionally required the frozen EEG artifact rules. These concurrent models were not interpreted as mediation or causal ordering.

Incremental prediction targeted each participant's theta-plus-minus-non-rhythmic inverse-efficiency contrast. Nested leave-one-participant-out ridge regression compared a baseline-feature model (individual theta frequency, baseline response time, age, and sex) with a model adding the ITPC contrast, evoked-SNR contrast, and internal consistency. Prediction error was evaluated against both the baseline-feature model and the training-fold mean using mean absolute error, root mean squared error, and participant-bootstrap uncertainty. The prediction gate required improvement over both comparators with uncertainty excluding no improvement; exploratory prediction could not rescue a failed primary or coupling result.

## 2.12 Statistical analysis

Resting EEG associations were estimated at the participant level. In ds004504, associations between spectral features and MMSE established the public reference pattern. Diagnostic-stage summaries were secondary to continuous associations because controls had an MMSE ceiling.

For the visual data, detectability was summarized by participant-level response distributions at each frequency. Diagnostic contrasts in the raw 10 Hz driving index used heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors. Because the distribution was right-skewed, the raw estimate was reported alongside prespecified log1p and 5th/95th-percentile winsorized sensitivity analyses. Frequency selectivity was tested with the within-participant 10 Hz-minus-other-frequencies contrast and checked against a repeated-frequency model. Associations with MMSE were estimated separately within AD and FTD to avoid treating the control-group ceiling as continuous severity information.

Resting-to-visual correspondence was tested in the participants shared by ds004504 and ds006036. Seven resting features were related to the eyes-open local response at four frequencies, with age, sex, and diagnosis included as covariates. The resulting 28 tests were controlled as one false-discovery-rate family. These models apply only to the defined eyes-open O1/O2 response and do not test all possible visual-response measures.

For the auditory data, target engagement was summarized by the participant-level stimulation-minus-resting 40 Hz response and the proportion of positive individual effects. Split-half stability and the monotonic association with MMSE were quantified with Spearman correlations. Network and temporal analyses were treated as exploratory and remained in the supplementary material after they failed the prespecified reliability or clinical-increment gates. Response-scale, reference and leave-one-subject-out checks are shown in Supplementary Figure S3, and all advanced multiplicity families are shown in Supplementary Figure S4.

All main results include the effective sample size, effect estimate, 95% confidence interval, and relevant model diagnostics. False-discovery-rate correction was retained for predefined feature families. Null-hypothesis tests were not interpreted as proof of no association. Where sensitivity bounds rather than established clinical minimum differences were available, equivalence analyses were labeled as design-sensitivity analyses rather than clinical equivalence tests. For ds006780, d-prime was standardized by its standard deviation in the technical sample, and symmetric bounds of 0.20, 0.25, and 0.30 outcome SD were evaluated separately for local log signal-to-noise ratio and inter-trial phase coherence. The two predictors were not pooled because only the inter-trial phase-coherence model was aligned with the corresponding neural reliability estimate.

Complete visual models, auditory models, and resting-to-visual models are reported in Supplementary Tables S3, S4, and S5, respectively. Blank multiplicity-adjusted cells indicate that an adjustment was not defined for that standalone analysis; they are not treated as zero.

# 3. Results

We first report cohort flow and dataset-specific findings, followed by tests of four predefined transitions from an observed result to a stronger mechanistic, cognitive, or predictive claim. Amplitudes and participants are not pooled across modalities or cohorts.

## 3.1 Public-data cohort flow and signal availability

All 88 resting EEG recordings in ds004504 passed the structural checks and were matched without ambiguity to the corresponding visual-stimulation participant identifiers in ds006036. In the resting data, the median proportion of 4-s windows retained after the amplitude-based quality screen was 99.47%. The visual dataset contained 351 blocks at 5, 10, 15, or 20 Hz with at least 2.5 s of reconstructed eyes-open exposure, yielding 311 participant-by-frequency observations (Supplementary Figure S1). The numbers of participants with usable eyes-open responses were 80, 76, 78, and 77 at 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz, respectively; 68 participants contributed all four frequencies. The more restrictive network analysis retained 85 participants with at least one eligible window. Its median analysis duration was 2.862 s per block, corresponding to a median of six frequency-domain observations.

All 35 auditory recordings in ds005048 passed the file, channel, sampling-rate, duration, event, and finite-value checks. Eight participants had six stimulation blocks and 27 had ten blocks; the first six blocks were available for every participant and defined the common analysis interval. MMSE was available for 33 participants. The advanced auditory analysis therefore included 210 40-s stimulation blocks, with a median of 38 s and 57 frequency-domain observations retained per block.

Figure 1 maps each cohort to the four claim-transition tests and summarizes the evidence used to judge each proposed upgrade. Matrix cells denote a direct test, contextual evidence or a data boundary, or no direct role; they do not encode effect direction or evidence strength. Outcome-specific effective sample sizes and the reasons why technical and behavioral samples differed are summarized separately in Figure 2.

## 3.2 Reference phenotypes, target engagement, and population sensitivity

### 3.2.1 Resting EEG captured a cognitive-impairment-related spectral axis

The public resting dataset showed the expected relationship between spectral slowing and cognitive status (Figure 4A). Posterior individual alpha peak frequency correlated positively with MMSE (Spearman ρ=0.401, p=1.08×10⁻⁴), whereas the spectral slowing ratio correlated negatively with MMSE (ρ=−0.515, p=2.76×10⁻⁷). The spectral exponent (ρ=−0.452, p=9.62×10⁻⁶), relative delta power (ρ=−0.384, p=2.20×10⁻⁴), and relative theta power (ρ=−0.511, p=3.55×10⁻⁷) were also negatively related to MMSE, while relative alpha power was positively related (ρ=0.431, p=2.75×10⁻⁵). These estimates establish that the resting feature pipeline recovered a disease-related spectral pattern in the public cohort. Because MMSE was fixed at 30 in the control group, the correlations were not interpreted as an unqualified linear disease continuum.

### 3.2.2 Eyes-open visual frequency-following responses were detectable but did not track MMSE

Eyes-open O1/O2 responses were positive in most participants at each stimulation frequency (Figure 3A). Median local signal-to-noise ratios were 4.659 dB at 5 Hz (64/80 participants above zero), 9.068 dB at 10 Hz (72/76), 8.777 dB at 15 Hz (76/78), and 8.206 dB at 20 Hz (70/77). The interquartile ranges were 1.211–7.060, 5.632–12.716, 5.882–11.474, and 4.559–11.648 dB, respectively. O1 and O2 had the largest or joint-largest median response at all four frequencies, consistent with an occipital-dominant scalp distribution (Supplementary Figure S2A). Applying a peak-to-peak threshold of 200 µV retained 334/351 blocks (95.2%) and left the frequency-specific medians materially unchanged.

The local response did not show a consistent monotonic relationship with MMSE. The unadjusted Spearman correlations were 0.087 at 5 Hz (p=0.441), −0.060 at 10 Hz (p=0.607), 0.043 at 15 Hz (p=0.707), and 0.081 at 20 Hz (p=0.482). Resting features also failed to predict the defined eyes-open local response after covariate adjustment and multiple-comparison control. Across seven resting predictors and four stimulation frequencies, the smallest false-discovery-rate-adjusted q value was 0.641. In particular, neither the distance between the individual alpha peak and 10 Hz (β=0.466 dB per SD, p=0.680) nor the resting slowing ratio (β=−0.024, p=0.971) predicted the 10 Hz local response.

### 3.2.3 Closed-eye 10 Hz selectivity differed by diagnosis but was sensitive to response scale

The closed-eye driving-index pipeline reproduced the features released with the directly competing analysis. Across 5, 10, 15, and 20 Hz, the Pearson correlation between locally generated and released features was at least 0.999978. This agreement establishes computational reproducibility of the feature extraction, not replication in an independent cohort.

At 10 Hz, the raw driving index was available for 80 participants. The means were 13.243 in cognitively normal controls (n=27), 6.493 in AD (n=32), and 6.618 in FTD (n=21); the corresponding medians were 9.520, 3.833, and 4.119. The large control-group standard deviation (16.053) relative to AD (6.337) and FTD (7.629) indicated marked right skew and sensitivity to high responders. In heteroskedasticity-consistent models, the adjusted raw-scale differences were −5.175 for AD versus controls (95% CI −10.730 to 0.381; p=0.0679) and −5.699 for FTD versus controls (95% CI −12.371 to 0.973; p=0.0941). After log1p transformation, the corresponding differences were −0.492 (95% CI −0.929 to −0.054; p=0.0276) and −0.595 (95% CI −1.136 to −0.053; p=0.0313). The raw and transformed estimates therefore agreed in direction but differed in inferential precision (Supplementary Figure S3A).

The participant-level frequency-selectivity contrast gave a more specific result (Figure 4B). Relative to the mean driving index at 5, 15, and 20 Hz, 10 Hz selectivity was lower in AD than in controls by 6.610 units (95% CI −11.876 to −1.345; p=0.0139) and lower in FTD by 7.174 units (95% CI −13.715 to −0.632; p=0.0316). A four-frequency random-intercept model produced similar 10 Hz contrasts, but the random-effect covariance was singular. The selectivity model therefore carries the primary group-level interpretation, with the repeated-frequency model treated as a sensitivity analysis.

All visual direct, transformed, selectivity, mixed, within-group severity, advanced, and reference-sensitivity results are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

## 3.3 State robustness, within-recording reliability, and individual clinical validity

### 3.3.1 The visual group effect did not establish individual clinical validity

Within the patient groups, the closed-eye 10 Hz driving index did not show a stable monotonic relationship with MMSE (Figure 4C). Spearman correlations were 0.125 in AD (95% bootstrap CI −0.233 to 0.442; n=32; p=0.497) and −0.237 in FTD (95% CI −0.611 to 0.221; n=21; p=0.302). Both intervals remained compatible with non-trivial associations. The directly competing analysis reported a split-half ICC of 0.06 and no improvement in diagnostic classification after the photic-driving features were added to resting EEG. Thus, the same response definition that produced a frequency-selective diagnostic-group contrast did not satisfy the available reliability, within-group severity, or incremental-value criteria for an individual biomarker.

The eyes-open and closed-eye analyses did not support a single state-invariant visual response effect. Eyes-open O1/O2 decibel signal-to-noise ratio was broadly detectable but showed no stable diagnostic or severity gradient, whereas the closed-eye posterior driving index showed a selective 10 Hz group difference. Eye state, analysis window, region of interest, and response denominator changed together, so their contributions could not be separated in this dataset.

### 3.3.2 Auditory 40 Hz target engagement was stable within recordings but unrelated to MMSE

The auditory analysis showed a positive stimulation-minus-resting response in 32/35 participants (Figure 3A). The median narrowband 40 Hz signal-to-noise ratio increase was 1.345 dB (range −0.707 to 6.179 dB), with a broadly distributed sensor-level response (Supplementary Figure S2B). Participant rankings were stable across the first and second halves of the six common stimulation blocks (Spearman ρ=0.838 for the foundation-analysis response; Figure 3B). In the advanced analysis, the frontocentral response retained a split-half correlation of 0.752. These coefficients describe within-recording stability; they do not estimate between-day test–retest reliability.

Among the 33 participants with MMSE, the correlation between the narrowband 40 Hz response and MMSE was ρ=0.085 (95% bootstrap CI −0.283 to 0.417; p=0.637; Figure 4D). This interval was too wide to support equivalence to zero. The response also failed to show an ordinal diagnostic-stage pattern: the descriptive median was 1.829 dB in the normal group, 2.492 dB in MCI, and 0.871 dB in mild AD. Given the small and unbalanced groups, the diagnostic-label/MMSE discordance, and a single participant with moderate AD, these summaries were not used to infer a stage trajectory.

All auditory adjusted, reference-sensitivity, amplitude-threshold, leave-one-subject-out, and within-recording split-half results are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

### 3.3.3 Network and temporal measures did not add clinical information

Visual network and temporal candidates were constrained by the short usable windows. Participant rankings for posterior–frontal imaginary coherence were weakly consistent across reference schemes (Supplementary Figure S3B), and none of the prespecified patient-severity models survived false-discovery-rate correction (minimum q=0.870). The primary diagnostic models had a minimum q of 0.800. Thirty-two exploratory models relating resting features to advanced visual measures also failed to survive correction (minimum q=0.110; Supplementary Figure S4). The overlap between nominal early and late 2-s windows had a median of 1.138 s, precluding a strong interpretation of within-block adaptation.

The 28 basic resting-to-visual models and 32 advanced models are reported as separate false-discovery-rate families in Supplementary Table S5.

The longer auditory blocks supported more stable estimation of some advanced measures. Split-half correlations were 0.591 for the central-minus-frontal signal-to-noise gradient and 0.529 for current-source-density-transformed central–frontal absolute imaginary coherence. After adjustment for age, sex, and the local frontocentral response, neither measure was associated with MMSE: the coefficients per 1-SD higher MMSE were −0.059 for the spatial gradient (p=0.810, q=0.811) and −0.00063 for imaginary coherence (p=0.811, q=0.811). The split-half correlations were −0.191 for the fourth-minus-first-quarter response and −0.204 for the within-block slope. Reference and leave-one-subject-out checks are shown in Supplementary Figure S3C–D. No exploratory auditory connectivity or adaptation measure passed false-discovery-rate correction; the minimum q was 0.883 across the full exploratory family and 0.995 in the subset testing incremental value beyond the local response (Supplementary Figure S4A).

## 3.4 Matched behavioral validity and held-out incremental prediction

### 3.4.1 Aggregate reliability did not guarantee matched concurrent validity

The ds007648 release contained 22 participants and 10,258 behavioral trials. Two recordings were excluded from neural analyses because the released signal files were truncated or frame-misaligned; the remaining 20 participants contributed 8,579 trials to the primary accuracy analysis and 7,456 correct trials to the response-time analysis. These exclusions were based on file integrity and signal availability rather than behavioral performance or response magnitude.

Participant rankings were stable for both aggregate frequency-tagged responses. Odd–even trial correlations, corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula, were 0.870 for 36 Hz occipital inter-trial phase coherence and 0.985 for 40 Hz central inter-trial phase coherence (Figure 3B). In matched participant-level analyses, 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence was not clearly associated with aggregate accuracy (Spearman ρ=−0.056, 95% participant-bootstrap CI −0.511 to 0.435; p=0.816) or median correct-trial response time (ρ=0.113, 95% CI −0.350 to 0.515; p=0.636). The intervals were wide because only 20 participants contributed neural data.

Aggregate behavioral rankings were also stable within the recording. Spearman–Brown-corrected reliability was 0.912 for participant-level accuracy (95% participant-bootstrap CI 0.730 to 0.971) and 0.991 for median correct-trial response time (95% CI 0.950 to 0.999). Thus, the aggregate neural and behavioral reliability coefficients and the aggregate concurrent-validity estimates refer to the same between-participant level.

The trial-level analysis addressed a different estimand. The leave-one-trial-out 40 Hz phase-projected score was not associated with accuracy (β=−0.0437 log-odds per within-participant SD, 95% CI −0.1071 to 0.0197; p=0.177, q=0.353) or correct-trial log response time (β=−0.00179, 95% CI −0.01497 to 0.01139; p=0.790, q=0.790). Aggregate inter-trial phase-coherence reliability cannot be assigned to this trial-state predictor. Target-modality interactions and amplitude-based alternatives did not alter the trial-level result.

In contrast, greater trial-level alignment with the participant-specific 36 Hz phase was associated with higher subsequent accuracy (β=0.1148 log-odds per within-participant SD, 95% CI 0.0466 to 0.1830; p=0.00097, q=0.00386), corresponding to an odds ratio of approximately 1.12 per SD (Figure 5A–C). The estimate was similar under a stricter ocular-artifact rule and after exclusion of the participant with late-recording noise. The 36 Hz association was not clearly specific to visual targets, was not reproduced by the amplitude measure, and did not meet the corrected threshold for response time. It therefore identifies a concurrent phase-state association rather than an effect of experimentally increasing 36 Hz response strength.

Leave-one-participant-out prediction did not convert either frequency-tagged signal into useful incremental prediction (Figure 6C). Adding the neural measures changed accuracy log loss by −0.000238 (95% bootstrap CI −0.000711 to 0.000309) and log-response-time mean absolute error by 0.000052 (95% CI −0.000235 to 0.000327). Relative to the corresponding baseline errors, these differences were −0.067% (95% CI −0.201% to 0.088%) and 0.012% (95% CI −0.052% to 0.073%), respectively. Thus, the reliable 40 Hz response did not show detectable concurrent behavioral validity in this task, while the 36 Hz concurrent association did not produce detectable out-of-participant predictive gain.

### 3.4.2 Developmental sensitivity was separable from matched cognitive validity

Of 123 participants with auditory steady-state recordings in ds006780, 111 met the signal-based criterion of at least 100 valid standard trials at both 27 and 40 Hz; 109 also had full-scale IQ data. The technical sample comprised 54 autistic participants, 33 typically developing participants, and 24 unaffected siblings. Odd–even trial correlations, corrected to full length, were 0.951 at 27 Hz and 0.972 at 40 Hz.

Reconstructed d-prime was likewise reliable within the session. Spearman–Brown-corrected coefficients were 0.916 for the 27 Hz standard condition (95% participant-bootstrap CI 0.863 to 0.949) and 0.931 for the 40 Hz standard condition (95% CI 0.884 to 0.959). The split preserved the exact full-task hit, miss, false-alarm, and correct-rejection totals used in the primary analysis.

The metric-matched validity analysis used 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence, which had a full-length reliability of 0.972, and the reliable 40 Hz d-prime outcome. After adjustment for 27 Hz inter-trial phase coherence, age, sex, and diagnostic group, the coefficient was 0.058 d-prime units per 1-SD higher 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence (95% CI −0.255 to 0.370; p=0.717). On the standardized outcome scale, the estimate was 0.047 SD per neural-predictor SD (90% CI −0.167 to 0.261). The interval excluded effects of 0.30 outcome SD or larger under the non-clinical sensitivity grid (TOST p=0.026), but did not exclude 0.25-SD effects (p=0.059). This directly aligned analysis did not establish concurrent validity despite high within-recording reliability of both variables, but its precision did not support a strict null interpretation.

The analysis recovered the expected developmental effect (Figure 3D). Per 1-SD increase in age, 40 Hz local log signal-to-noise ratio increased by 2.258 dB (95% CI 1.104 to 3.413; p=0.000126, q=0.00114), and 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence increased by 0.0753 (95% CI 0.0421 to 0.1085; p=8.80×10⁻⁶, q=0.000158). Adjusted diagnostic-group differences did not survive multiplicity correction.

Developmental sensitivity did not imply concurrent task validity (Figure 5D). In the prespecified model controlling for 27 Hz response, age, sex, and diagnostic group, the standardized 40 Hz local log signal-to-noise ratio was unrelated to 40 Hz d-prime (β=−0.004, 95% CI −0.313 to 0.305; p=0.979, q=0.979). Its standardized 90% interval was −0.215 to 0.208 outcome SD, excluding effects of 0.25 SD or larger under the sensitivity grid (TOST p=0.027). Morlet power and inter-trial phase coherence gave similarly imprecise estimates. The inter-trial phase-coherence result is the metric-matched reliability–validity test; the local signal-to-noise ratio result does not inherit inter-trial phase-coherence reliability or its sensitivity bound. Repeated cross-validation showed higher prediction error after neural features were added: mean absolute error increased by 0.0146 (95% bootstrap CI 0.0060 to 0.0232), and root mean squared error increased by 0.0200 (95% CI 0.0116 to 0.0286). These changes represented relative deteriorations of 1.48% (95% CI 0.61% to 2.36%) and 1.70% (95% CI 0.99% to 2.45%), respectively.

An exploratory conditional association with full-scale IQ required a separate interpretation (Figure 6A–B). In the full technically usable release, a 1-SD higher 40 Hz local log signal-to-noise ratio was associated with a 5.13-point lower full-scale IQ after the same adjustment set (95% CI −9.49 to −0.78; p=0.0209, q=0.0418). The direction was consistent for Morlet power and inter-trial phase coherence. However, the local signal-to-noise estimate weakened to −2.98 points (95% CI −7.12 to 1.17; p=0.159) when the analysis was restricted to participants with full-scale IQ above 80, and to −2.49 points (95% CI −6.64 to 1.66; p=0.239) under the combined age and IQ eligibility range. Adding the neural features did not clearly improve out-of-sample full-scale IQ prediction. The association therefore depended on the released sample's ability range and did not qualify as a transportable cognitive marker.

## 3.5 Mean active-control behavioral effect, within-participant coupling, and individual selection

### 3.5.1 Personalized theta stimulation produced reliable target engagement and an acute performance advantage without condition-specific neural–behavioral coupling

The Dryad personalized-theta cohort included 44 adults aged 60–75 years who completed individualized theta-frequency stimulation, stimulation at 1.33 times the individualized frequency, fixed 2 Hz stimulation, and a non-rhythmic condition. Analyses of the released participant-level table reproduced an active-control advantage: the mean of the two personalized conditions was 10.56 ms faster than 2 Hz stimulation (95% CI −16.25 to −4.86; p=0.000544; paired d<sub>z</sub>=−0.563). The individualized and 1.33-times-individualized conditions differed by −1.28 ms (95% CI −5.20 to 2.64; p=0.515). Accuracy was 0.43 percentage points lower under the personalized conditions (95% CI −0.85 to −0.003 percentage points; p=0.0483), but the inverse-efficiency difference remained favorable (−8.44 ms, 95% CI −14.21 to −2.68; p=0.00510). The response-time advantage therefore persisted after an accuracy correction, although a small speed–accuracy contribution could not be excluded.

Decomposing the released entrainment measure separated between-participant and within-participant associations. After adjustment for condition, baseline response time, age, and sex, a 1-SD higher participant-level mean entrainment value was associated with a 12.25-ms faster post-stimulation response (95% CI −20.16 to −4.33; p=0.00242). The condition-specific deviation from each participant's mean was −0.90 ms per SD (95% CI −3.08 to 1.28; p=0.417), and the same within-participant estimate remained imprecise in a participant-fixed-effect model and a 20,000-permutation test (p=0.402). The released measure may therefore identify a stable between-participant response tendency, but it did not provide evidence that condition-to-condition increases mediated the behavioral advantage.

Claims about differential benefit also depended on the comparator. Baseline response time correlated with improvement relative to the participant's own baseline (r=0.349, 95% CI 0.058 to 0.585; p=0.020) and with advantage over the non-rhythmic condition (r=−0.475, 95% CI −0.676 to −0.207; p=0.00112), but not with the personalized advantage over active 2 Hz stimulation (r=−0.133, 95% CI −0.414 to 0.170; p=0.389). A nested leave-one-participant-out ridge model using pretreatment age, sex, baseline response time, and individualized theta frequency had a mean absolute error of 14.64 ms, compared with 14.56 ms for the training-set-mean benchmark (difference 0.08 ms, 95% bootstrap CI −0.32 to 0.49), a relative difference of 0.57% (95% CI −2.18% to 3.39%). These released-table analyses did not support a deployable individual-benefit rule and motivated the independently reconstructed raw-EEG tests below.

In the frozen 35-participant confirmatory cohort, frontocentral ITPC at the common analysis frequency was higher after theta-plus than after non-rhythmic stimulation (mean paired difference 0.088, 95% CI 0.051 to 0.124; paired d<sub>z</sub>=0.830; two-sided p=2.23×10<sup>−5</sup>; Figure 7A). The estimate was unchanged when at least four good frontocentral channels were required. Evoked local log SNR was also higher by 7.518 dB (95% CI 4.522 to 10.514; d<sub>z</sub>=0.862; Holm-adjusted p=3.83×10<sup>−5</sup>), whereas total-power (0.306 dB, 95% CI −0.097 to 0.709; Holm-adjusted p=0.264) and induced-power (−0.097 dB, 95% CI −0.393 to 0.200; Holm-adjusted p=0.512) contrasts crossed zero. This dissociation localized the regularity effect to phase-consistent activity under the tested decomposition; it does not distinguish endogenous entrainment from repeated or temporally predictable evoked responses.

ITPC showed high within-recording internal consistency in both theta-plus (Spearman–Brown coefficients 0.956 for odd–even and 0.961 for time-half splits) and non-rhythmic conditions (0.964 and 0.946, respectively). Evoked-SNR consistency was lower in theta-plus (0.855 and 0.717) and weak and imprecise in the non-rhythmic condition (0.135 and 0.236). These coefficients characterize within-session participant ordering and do not establish between-day stability.

Theta-plus stimulation reduced median correct response time by 27.727 ms relative to the non-rhythmic control (95% CI −36.171 to −19.284; d<sub>z</sub>=−1.128; Holm-adjusted p=3.49×10<sup>−7</sup>) and reduced inverse efficiency by 25.856 ms (95% CI −35.455 to −16.257; d<sub>z</sub>=−0.925; Holm-adjusted p=8.31×10<sup>−6</sup>; Figure 7B). The accuracy difference was −0.006 (95% CI −0.014 to 0.002; Holm-adjusted p=0.119), providing no detected accuracy decrement but not a formal non-inferiority result.

The stronger neural response did not establish condition-specific neural–behavioral coupling (Figure 7C). For leave-one-trial-out phase alignment centered within participant and condition, correctness slopes were −0.098 in non-rhythmic trials (95% CI −1.187 to 0.991) and −0.072 in theta-plus trials (95% CI −0.879 to 0.735); correct-log-response-time slopes were 0.006 (95% CI −0.029 to 0.042) and −0.004 (95% CI −0.026 to 0.019), respectively. All Holm-adjusted p values were 1.000. These estimates neither supported concurrent coupling nor tested mediation.

Adding the frozen neural features did not consistently improve nested leave-one-participant-out prediction of inverse-efficiency benefit (Figure 7D). Relative to the training-fold mean, the mean absolute-error improvement was 3.874 ms (95% bootstrap CI −0.431 to 8.032) and the root-mean-square-error improvement was 2.793 ms. Relative to the baseline-feature model, the corresponding improvements were −1.024 ms (95% CI −3.207 to 1.013) and 0.225 ms. Because improvement over both comparators was not established, the prespecified prediction gate did not pass. This is a sample-bounded failure of incremental prediction, not evidence that individual benefit is intrinsically unpredictable.

## 3.6 Cross-cohort tests of four inferential transitions

The seven datasets provided four design-based tests of whether evidence at one level justified a stronger claim (Table 1). First, the visual data showed that a detectable or diagnostically sensitive frequency-following response was conditional on eye state, response scale, spatial summary, and denominator; the available evidence did not support a state-general endogenous-entrainment interpretation. Second, two independent analyses paired high within-recording neural reliability with weak or imprecise metric-matched cognitive validity: auditory 40 Hz signal-to-noise ratio versus MMSE in ds005048, and 40 Hz inter-trial phase coherence versus 40 Hz d-prime in ds006780. Third, the personalized-theta cohort showed a mean advantage over active 2 Hz stimulation without corresponding evidence for condition-level neural–behavioral coupling. Fourth, in-sample associations in ds007648, ds006780, and the personalized-theta cohort did not consistently improve held-out prediction. These results constrain the four proposed upgrades without negating the positive evidence at their preceding levels.

# 4. Discussion

Across seven public human EEG datasets, rhythmic sensory responses were usually detectable and often stable within a recording, but stronger interpretations required separate tests. Four recurrent breaks appeared in the evidential chain: detectability or group sensitivity did not establish a state-general mechanism; within-recording reliability did not establish matched cognitive validity; a mean active-control behavioral advantage did not establish within-participant neural–behavioral coupling; and an in-sample association did not establish incremental prediction for new participants. These are bounded counterexamples to automatic claim upgrading, not evidence that rhythmic stimulation lacks physiological or behavioral effects.

The visual findings show why a group contrast is insufficient for individual biomarker qualification. Closed-eye 10-Hz selectivity differed by diagnostic group, yet the raw-scale interval was imprecise, the reported split-half intraclass correlation was 0.06, and within-AD and within-FTD MMSE associations were weak and directionally inconsistent. The eyes-open O1/O2 response did not reproduce that diagnostic pattern. Because the analyses differed simultaneously in eye state, interval, region, scale, and denominator, the contrast cannot be attributed to eye closure alone. It instead establishes that state and response definition must be fixed before a stimulation marker is evaluated. A stimulus-frequency peak alone remains compatible with repeated evoked activity or temporal predictability rather than endogenous-oscillator entrainment [@L001_Duecker2024; @L007_Duecker2021; @L041_Breska2017].

The linked resting-to-stimulation analysis further separated disease-associated spontaneous activity from the measured visual response. Resting slowing, alpha features, and spectral exponent tracked MMSE, but did not predict the defined eyes-open response after adjustment and multiplicity control. This does not establish biological independence; it shows that a disease-related resting feature cannot be assumed to predict a stimulation response without a direct, prespecified test.

The auditory cohorts provided complementary boundaries. Older-adult 40-Hz responses were stable within recordings, yet response magnitude, spatial gradient, imaginary coherence, and adaptation did not add MMSE information. In the developmental cohort, reliable 40-Hz inter-trial phase coherence and d-prime coexisted with an imprecise adjusted association. In the cross-modal attention cohort, aggregate 40-Hz phase coherence was reliable but showed near-zero aggregate behavioral associations; a separate trial-state association was interpreted only at that within-participant level. Absolute amplitudes cannot be compared across cohorts because participants, stimuli, systems, references, and definitions differed. Their shared contribution is narrower: reliability did not guarantee concurrent or predictive validity when the metric and aggregation level were matched.

The published ds006222 source study provides complementary positive evidence: its 40-Hz audiovisual group showed better vigilance accuracy and response time than constant-light and random-flicker groups, with exploratory associations involving delta power and lower-alpha connectivity [@L044_Attokaren2026]. The public release available here omitted the separate PsychoPy outcomes required to reconstruct those behavioral exclusions and contrasts. We therefore did not recode unavailable endpoints as null findings or present the published associations as independently reproduced. This mismatch defines a reproducibility boundary: open EEG can permit target-engagement checks while remaining insufficient for independent qualification of the linked behavioral endpoint.

The frozen personalized-theta analysis showed positive neural and behavioral effects within the same cohort. Temporal regularity produced a large frontocentral phase-locking difference, a selectively evoked spectral response, and faster performance with improved inverse efficiency. Nevertheless, trial-to-trial phase alignment did not track correctness or response time within either condition, and participant-level neural features did not consistently improve held-out prediction. Target engagement, acute performance, concurrent coupling, and prediction therefore separated in the same participants. The absence of supported coupling does not negate the neural or behavioral effects; it limits the claim that the measured phase response was their condition-specific behavioral mechanism.

This result empirically complements the distinction between physiological resonance and artificial superimposition [@L043_Qi2026]. The selective evoked response is compatible with phase-consistent stimulus following, but the design cannot establish endogenous resonance, deep-brain engagement, or the proposed biological effects of repeated 40-Hz treatment. Mechanism specificity and cognitive-marker validity are related but non-interchangeable requirements. Comparator choice likewise mattered: slower baseline performance related to change from baseline and advantage over a non-rhythmic condition, but not to added benefit over active 2-Hz stimulation. An average active-control effect did not yield a validated rule for selecting stimulation for a new participant.

Therapeutic interpretation remains correspondingly limited. OVERTURE produced EEG-verified target engagement without separation on its principal cognitive outcomes, although a selected imaging subset produced structural signals [@L020_L027_Hajos2024; @L035_Da2024]. The same endpoint-level tension appeared in meta-analysis [@L013_Ang2025]. These results cannot qualify EEG synchronization as a surrogate. The HOPE pivotal trial had completed follow-up but had not posted results at the literature cutoff [@L036_HOPE], and a randomized 40-Hz versus non-40-Hz music trial with EEG primary outcomes remained in recruitment [@L042_GammaAuditoryTrial]. The present datasets include acute behavior but no longitudinal clinical outcome. They support requirements for future qualification—state-robust engagement, between-day reliability, prespecified within-participant clinical coupling, and external prediction—rather than a chronic-efficacy conclusion.

The cohorts do not represent a single disease continuum. AD and FTD share cognitive impairment and resting spectral slowing but differ in pathology, topography, and clinical phenotype; cognitively normal older adults additionally differ from patients in disease burden and cognitive range. We therefore confined disease-related estimates to linked AD, FTD, and control recordings and used independent healthy-aging and developmental cohorts only to test measurement properties and inferential transitions. Cross-cohort convergence concerns claim qualification, not a pooled biological effect. This design respects heterogeneity but cannot establish that the same transition failures have identical causes across populations.

Event timing was reconstructed from hardware and eye-state markers; technical exclusions were separated from behavioral outcomes; and response-scale, reference, artifact, eligibility-range, comparator, complete-case, and behavioral-reliability sensitivities were retained. Group effects, within-group severity, stability, concurrent association, active-control benefit, coupling, and prediction were treated as distinct estimands. The 35-participant raw-EEG confirmation was frozen before condition-level results were accessed, and its evoked, total, and induced measures were separated.

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. These are secondary analyses; only the Dryad raw-EEG confirmation was frozen before confirmatory signal re-access. MMSE is coarse and showed ceiling effects or diagnostic discordance in some cohorts. Split-half coefficients do not replace between-day reliability. Trial-level associations remain vulnerable to time-varying common causes, and null intervals do not prove that every neural–behavioral relationship is absent. The acute personalized-theta cohort comprised healthy older adults rather than patients with cognitive impairment. The data do not support anatomical source, directed propagation, endogenous entrainment, chronic efficacy, disease modification, or patient treatment selection.

Human rhythmic-sensory EEG responses were measurable, but their meaning depended on state, metric, comparator, population, and claim. A diagnostic-group contrast could coexist with low individual reliability; a reliable response could lack cognitive validity; and an average active-control benefit could coexist with unsupported condition-level coupling and individual prediction. Sensory target engagement is therefore an initial observation, not by itself a cognitive biomarker, treatment-selection marker, or clinical surrogate.

## Figure 7

**Figure 7. Target engagement, acute task-performance advantage, neural–behavioral coupling, and individual-benefit prediction are separable claims.** Results are from the frozen raw-EEG cohort of 35 healthy older adults and compare theta-plus with non-rhythmic stimulation. **A**, Participant-level paired differences in frontocentral inter-trial phase coherence at the common analysis frequency. The mean difference was 0.088 (95% CI 0.051 to 0.124; paired d<sub>z</sub>=0.830; p=2.23×10<sup>−5</sup>). Evoked local log signal-to-noise ratio increased by 7.518 dB, whereas total- and induced-power contrasts crossed zero, localizing the effect to phase-consistent activity under the tested decomposition. **B**, Paired behavioral differences. Median correct response time was 27.727 ms lower (95% CI 19.284 to 36.171 ms lower), inverse efficiency was 25.856 ms lower (95% CI 16.257 to 35.455 ms lower), and the accuracy interval crossed zero. **C**, Condition-specific slopes of leave-one-trial-out phase alignment for correctness and correct-trial log response time. All four intervals crossed zero and all Holm-adjusted p values were 1.000. **D**, Nested leave-one-participant-out incremental prediction of inverse-efficiency benefit. Neural features improved mean absolute error by 3.874 ms relative to the training-fold mean (95% bootstrap CI −0.431 to 8.032) but worsened it by 1.024 ms relative to the baseline-feature model (95% CI −3.207 to 1.013); the prespecified prediction gate did not pass. The active-control contrast supports stimulus-locked target engagement and an acute task-performance advantage, but not condition-specific neural–behavioral coupling, mediation, a deployable treatment-selection rule, chronic efficacy, or disease modification.
